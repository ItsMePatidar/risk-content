# 3.2 Asset-Liability Management Basics

## Definition

**Asset-liability management (ALM)** is the practice of managing the mismatch between the timing of cash inflows from assets and outflows to liabilities. Its primary instrument is the **structural liquidity statement**, a table that places every expected cash inflow and outflow into a time bucket and reports the gap in each. Where IRRBB (2.2) buckets by repricing date, ALM for liquidity buckets by cash flow date - the two statements look similar and answer different questions. The measure supervisors examine is the **cumulative mismatch**, expressed as a percentage of cumulative outflows.

## Why it matters

A retail lender is structurally mismatched by design. Home loans run twenty years, personal loans three, and the funding runs two to three. Borrowing short to lend long is the business, not a defect - but it has to be sized and buffered. The structural liquidity statement is where the size becomes visible and where regulatory tolerance limits bite. RBI's liquidity risk management framework for NBFCs prescribes granular buckets of 1-7 days, 8-14 days, and 15-30 days, with cumulative negative mismatch capped at 10%, 10%, and 20% of cumulative cash outflows respectively. For banks, the ALM guidelines cap the negative gap in the near buckets at similar orders of magnitude. Breaching these is a supervisory event, not an internal metric.

## Formula

```
Mismatch_t = Inflows_t - Outflows_t

Cumulative mismatch_T = SUM over t=1 to T of Mismatch_t

Cumulative mismatch % = Cumulative mismatch_T / SUM over t=1 to T of Outflows_t
```

where:
- `Inflows_t` = expected cash inflows in bucket t, in ₹ - behaviourally adjusted for prepayment and collection efficiency
- `Outflows_t` = contractual cash outflows in bucket t, in ₹
- `t` = time bucket, with the near buckets defined granularly because that is where survival is decided

Bucketing conventions that matter in a retail book:

Amortising retail loans are spread across buckets according to the EMI schedule, not placed at final maturity. This is what makes retail assets liquidity-friendly relative to bullet exposures.

EMI due dates cluster. Most retail lenders concentrate mandates on the 1st, 5th, and 10th of the month to align with salary credits, so inflows are front-loaded within the month and the 15-30 day bucket is systematically thinner than a straight-line assumption suggests.

Credit card outstandings have no contractual maturity. They are bucketed on behavioural run-off derived from observed payment rates, typically producing an effective life of 9 to 18 months on revolving balances.

Undrawn commitments - home loan tranches under construction-linked disbursement, sanctioned but undisbursed personal loans - are outflows in the bucket where they are expected to be drawn.

## Worked Example

Example: the structural liquidity statement for the NBFC from 3.1, over the first 30 days, using RBI's granular buckets for NBFCs.

Statement excluding liquid assets:

| Bucket | Inflows | Outflows | Gap | Cumulative gap | Cumulative outflows | Cumulative mismatch % | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-7 days | ₹96 cr | ₹118 cr | (₹22 cr) | (₹22 cr) | ₹118 cr | (18.6%) | 10% |
| 8-14 days | ₹58 cr | ₹196 cr | (₹138 cr) | (₹160 cr) | ₹314 cr | (51.0%) | 10% |
| 15-30 days | ₹120 cr | ₹448 cr | (₹328 cr) | (₹488 cr) | ₹762 cr | (64.0%) | 20% |

Every bucket breaches. Worked for the second row:

```
Cumulative gap    = (₹22 cr) + (₹138 cr) = (₹160 cr)
Cumulative outflows = ₹118 cr + ₹196 cr  =  ₹314 cr
Cumulative mismatch % = -160 / 314 = -51.0%, against a 10% limit
```

This is the normal, unmitigated position of a wholesale-funded retail lender. The loan book simply does not return principal fast enough to cover contractual debt maturities in a 30-day window.

Now include the liquid asset buffer of ₹556 crore in the 1-7 day bucket, since government securities and bank balances are convertible within days:

| Bucket | Inflows | Outflows | Gap | Cumulative gap | Cumulative mismatch % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-7 days | ₹652 cr | ₹118 cr | ₹534 cr | ₹534 cr | +452.5% |
| 8-14 days | ₹58 cr | ₹196 cr | (₹138 cr) | ₹396 cr | +126.1% |
| 15-30 days | ₹120 cr | ₹448 cr | (₹328 cr) | ₹68 cr | +8.9% |

All three buckets are compliant, and the cumulative position at day 30 is positive by ₹68 crore. The statement has not changed the business model - it has demonstrated that the liquid asset buffer is doing all the work, and quantified how much of it is consumed. By day 30 the buffer is 88% used. That number, not the loan book, is what determines how long the lender survives, which is the calculation in 3.4.

Behavioural adjustment illustration. If home loan prepayments run at 14% annualised rather than the 6% assumed, the 15-30 day inflow rises by roughly ₹21 crore and the day-30 cumulative position improves to ₹89 crore. Prepayment is a liquidity benefit and an earnings loss at the same time - the reverse of its treatment in 2.2.

## How it connects

This statement is the cash-flow twin of the repricing gap in 2.2, and the two are frequently confused because they use similar tables. The liquid asset buffer that closes the gap here is the HQLA measured formally in 3.3. The consumption rate of that buffer under stress is the survival horizon in 3.4, and the bucket limits and stock ratios applied to the output are set out in 3.5. Collection efficiency on the inflow side is an output of the collections function in 1.12.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Bucketing amortising retail loans at final maturity. A 20-year home loan delivers cash every month; placing it in the over-5-year bucket makes the statement meaningless and is the single most common construction error.
- Using contractual inflows without behavioural adjustment. Collection efficiency below 100%, prepayments, and delinquency all move actual inflows away from schedule, and the adjustment is not symmetric.
- Assuming EMI inflows are evenly distributed within the month. Due-date clustering front-loads them, which flatters the 1-7 day bucket and starves the 15-30 day bucket.
- Ignoring undrawn commitments. Sanctioned-but-undisbursed home loan tranches are contractual outflows, and leaving them out understates the near-bucket gap.
- Reading a compliant statement as a safe position. The example above is compliant only because the buffer is counted as a day-one inflow. The statement says nothing about what happens if the buffer cannot be monetised at par.
