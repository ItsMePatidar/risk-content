# 3.1 Fundamentals of Funding Liquidity for a Lending Business

## Definition

**Funding liquidity risk** is the risk that a lender cannot meet its obligations as they fall due without incurring unacceptable cost. It is distinct from **market liquidity risk**, which is the risk that an asset cannot be sold quickly without a price concession, and distinct from solvency, which is about whether assets exceed liabilities at all. A retail lender can be entirely solvent - a performing loan book, positive net worth, healthy capital ratio - and still fail, because a retail loan book cannot be converted into cash on the timeline that a maturing borrowing demands.

## Why it matters

Liquidity kills faster than credit. Credit deterioration plays out over four to eight quarters and gives management time to reprice, tighten, and provision. A funding shock plays out in days. The 2018-19 Indian NBFC stress is the reference case: after the IL&FS default, the commercial paper market effectively closed to NBFCs, and lenders with performing retail books found they could not roll short-term paper that was funding long-dated assets. The books did not go bad first - the funding went away first, and forced asset sales and disbursement freezes did the rest.

The structural feature of a retail lending balance sheet cuts both ways. Retail assets throw off large, predictable monthly cash flow through EMI collections, which is a genuine liquidity strength compared with a bullet-repayment corporate loan. But the book cannot be liquidated at speed. Securitisation and direct assignment are the only real exit routes, both take four to eight weeks to execute, and both require a functioning market - which is exactly what is missing in the scenario where they are needed.

## Formula

The core measure is the net funding requirement over a horizon:

```
Net funding requirement = Contractual outflows + Committed disbursements - Expected inflows
```

where:
- `Contractual outflows` = scheduled debt repayments, interest, and operating expenses falling due in the horizon, in ₹
- `Committed disbursements` = loan disbursements the lender is contractually or commercially obliged to make, in ₹
- `Expected inflows` = EMI collections, prepayments, and maturing investments, adjusted for realistic collection efficiency, in ₹

The dependence on refinancing is captured as:

```
Rollover dependency = Borrowings maturing in the horizon / Total borrowings
```

Two distinctions matter throughout liquidity work. **Contractual cash flow** is what the documents say; **behavioural cash flow** is what actually happens - home loan prepayments arrive early, credit card revolving balances persist far beyond the statement cycle, and a share of EMI collections always slips. Liquidity measurement uses behavioural flows for assets and contractual flows for liabilities, because that is the conservative combination.

**Counterbalancing capacity** is the total of what the lender can convert to cash within the horizon: liquid assets, undrawn committed bank lines, and the realisable value of loan pools eligible for securitisation, each with a haircut and, critically, an execution timeline.

## Worked Example

Example: an NBFC with a ₹4,450 crore retail book - ₹1,840 crore of personal loans and ₹2,610 crore of home loans - funded by ₹4,010 crore of borrowings and ₹620 crore of Tier 1 capital.

Funding stack:

| Source | Amount | Share | Residual maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank term loans | ₹2,560 cr | 63.8% | 2.4 years average |
| Non-convertible debentures | ₹1,100 cr | 27.4% | 3.1 years average |
| Commercial paper | ₹350 cr | 8.7% | 68 days average |
| Total borrowings | ₹4,010 cr | 100% | |

Cash flows over the next 30 days:

```
Scheduled debt repayments and interest          = ₹412 cr
Commercial paper maturing                       = ₹350 cr
Committed home loan tranche disbursements       =  ₹64 cr
New personal loan disbursements (business plan) = ₹121 cr
Operating expenses                              =  ₹22 cr
                                                  ---------
Total outflows                                  = ₹969 cr

EMI collections (₹243 cr contractual at 97.2% efficiency) = ₹236 cr
Maturing treasury investments                             =  ₹31 cr
                                                            ---------
Total inflows                                             = ₹267 cr

Net funding requirement = ₹969 cr - ₹267 cr = ₹702 cr
```

Against liquid assets of ₹556 crore, the NBFC is ₹146 crore short on a 30-day view even before any stress. The business plan disbursements of ₹121 crore are discretionary and can be cut, which closes most of the gap - but that is a decision to shrink the franchise, taken under duress.

Note the concentration point separately. Commercial paper is only 8.7% of borrowings, but 100% of it matures inside 68 days, and it accounts for half of the 30-day outflow. Rollover dependency for the month is ₹762 crore of maturing debt against ₹4,010 crore total, or 19.0% - meaning the NBFC must refinance roughly a fifth of its entire liability stack every month, in a market that is open to it only while sentiment holds.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Confusing liquidity with solvency. Every failed NBFC in 2018-19 was solvent on the day it stopped paying. Capital adequacy tells you nothing about whether cash will arrive in time.
- Treating a performing loan book as a liquid asset. It generates cash monthly and can be sold in weeks, not days, and only when buyers exist.
- Measuring rollover dependency on a full-year view. The relevant question is what matures in the next 30 to 90 days, because that is the window in which a market can stay closed.
- Counting undrawn bank lines as certain. Revocable lines are withdrawn precisely when the borrower's credit deteriorates. Only irrevocable committed facilities belong in counterbalancing capacity.
- Assuming collection efficiency holds in stress. A funding crisis is usually accompanied by a credit cycle turn, and EMI inflows fall at the same moment refinancing becomes hard.
