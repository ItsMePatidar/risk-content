# 3.3 Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)

## Definition

The **Liquidity Coverage Ratio** requires a lender to hold enough **high-quality liquid assets (HQLA)** to cover its net cash outflows over a 30-day acute stress scenario. It is a single ratio with a hard minimum of 100%, and it is the main Pillar 1 liquidity standard under Basel III. The stress scenario is prescribed, not chosen: run-off rates on liabilities and inflow rates on assets are set by the regulator, so two lenders with identical balance sheets report identical LCRs. This is deliberate - the ratio is designed for comparability rather than for accuracy about any particular firm.

## Why it matters

For a retail lending book, the LCR has two features that shape behaviour more than the headline number does. First, EMI collections - the lender's most reliable cash inflow - are only partially recognised. Under the bank framework, amounts due from retail counterparties are given a 50% inflow rate, and total inflows are capped at 75% of total outflows regardless of how large they are. A lender cannot pass the LCR on the strength of its collections. Second, undrawn credit card limits and committed but undisbursed loan sanctions are outflows, so growth in sanctioned-but-unused retail credit consumes liquidity capacity even though no money has left the building.

In India the ratio applies to all commercial banks and, under Scale Based Regulation, to deposit-taking NBFCs and larger non-deposit-taking NBFCs, phased to a 100% minimum. From 1 April 2026, RBI's revised bank LCR guidelines apply an additional 2.5 percentage point run-off factor to retail and small-business deposits enabled for internet and mobile banking, taking stable IMB-enabled deposits to 7.5% and less stable to 12.5%.

## Formula

```
LCR = Stock of HQLA / Total net cash outflows over 30 days  >= 100%

Total net cash outflows = Total expected outflows - min(Total expected inflows, 75% x Total expected outflows)
```

where:
- `HQLA` = unencumbered assets convertible to cash at little or no loss of value, in ₹
- `Total expected outflows` = each liability and off-balance-sheet commitment multiplied by its prescribed run-off or draw-down factor, in ₹
- `Total expected inflows` = each contractual receivable multiplied by its prescribed inflow rate, in ₹
- The 75% cap prevents a lender from relying entirely on inflows and holding no buffer

HQLA is tiered. Level 1 - cash, central bank reserves, and government securities - is counted at market value with prescribed haircuts and has no cap. Level 2A and 2B assets carry larger haircuts and are capped at 40% and 15% of total HQLA respectively.

Prescribed factors relevant to a retail balance sheet, under the bank framework:

| Item | Factor |
|---|---|
| Stable retail deposits, IMB-enabled | 7.5% run-off |
| Less stable retail deposits, IMB-enabled | 12.5% run-off |
| Unsecured wholesale funding, non-financial corporates | 40% run-off |
| Unsecured wholesale funding, financial institutions | 100% run-off |
| Undrawn committed facilities to retail and small business | 5% draw-down |
| Amounts due from retail counterparties (EMI collections) | 50% inflow |
| Amounts due from financial institutions | 100% inflow |

For NBFCs, RBI's framework applies a stress overlay directly: expected outflows are increased by 15% and expected inflows reduced by 25%, with the same 75% cap on inflows.

## Worked Example

Example: LCR for the NBFC from 3.1, using the NBFC stress convention.

Expected outflows over 30 days:

```
Scheduled debt repayments and interest       = ₹412 cr
Commercial paper maturing                    = ₹350 cr
Committed home loan tranche disbursements    =  ₹64 cr
Operating expenses                           =  ₹22 cr
                                               ---------
Expected outflows                            = ₹848 cr
Stressed outflows = ₹848 cr x 1.15           = ₹975 cr
```

Business plan disbursements of ₹121 crore are excluded because they are discretionary and not contractually committed.

Expected inflows over 30 days:

```
EMI collections (behaviourally adjusted)     = ₹236 cr
Maturing treasury investments                =  ₹31 cr
                                               ---------
Expected inflows                             = ₹267 cr
Stressed inflows = ₹267 cr x 0.75            = ₹200 cr
Cap check: 75% x ₹975 cr = ₹731 cr - cap not binding
```

Net cash outflows and ratio:

```
Net cash outflows = ₹975 cr - ₹200 cr = ₹775 cr

HQLA:
  Cash and bank balances       =  ₹96 cr
  Treasury bills               = ₹120 cr
  Government securities        = ₹340 cr
                                 --------
  Total Level 1 HQLA           = ₹556 cr

LCR = ₹556 cr / ₹775 cr = 71.7%
```

The NBFC fails, against a 100% minimum. The shortfall is ₹219 crore of HQLA, or equivalently ₹219 crore of net outflow that needs to be removed.

Remediation. The single largest driver is the ₹350 crore of commercial paper, which is 41% of gross outflows. Refinancing it with three-year NCDs removes it entirely from the 30-day window:

```
Expected outflows          = ₹848 cr - ₹350 cr = ₹498 cr
Stressed outflows          = ₹498 cr x 1.15    = ₹573 cr
Stressed inflows           = ₹200 cr
Cap check: 75% x ₹573 cr   = ₹430 cr - not binding
Net cash outflows          = ₹373 cr

LCR = ₹556 cr / ₹373 cr = 149.1%
```

Terming out one funding line moves the ratio from 71.7% to 149.1% without adding a rupee of HQLA. That is the mechanism through which the LCR pushes lenders toward longer-dated funding, and it is the intended effect of the rule.

Note what the EMI collections contributed. ₹236 crore of contractual retail inflows was recognised as ₹200 crore after the stress haircut - and in the original configuration, that ₹200 crore reduced a ₹975 crore outflow by 20%. A lender cannot solve an LCR problem on the asset side.

## How it connects

HQLA is the same liquid asset buffer that closed the structural liquidity gap in 3.2, now measured under a prescribed stress rather than a behavioural one. The government securities in HQLA are the trading book portfolio that carried market risk capital in 2.4 - the same assets serve a liquidity purpose and a market risk exposure simultaneously. The 30-day horizon here is short; the survival horizon calculation in 3.4 extends it, and the Indian regulatory perimeter for who must compute the ratio is set out in 3.5.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Assuming EMI collections solve the ratio. They are haircut and then capped at 75% of outflows. A lender with enormous retail inflows and no buffer still fails.
- Counting encumbered securities as HQLA. Assets pledged for borrowings, margin, or repo are excluded, and in practice a meaningful share of an NBFC's government securities are already pledged.
- Ignoring undrawn commitments. Sanctioned home loan tranches and undrawn credit card limits generate outflows under the framework even when historically they are rarely drawn in a 30-day window.
- Treating LCR as a measure of liquidity risk. It is a standardised 30-day snapshot with prescribed assumptions. It does not measure the lender's actual behaviour under its actual funding structure, which is what 3.4 is for.
- Overlooking the interaction with NSFR. The Net Stable Funding Ratio addresses the same mismatch over a one-year horizon, and a lender can pass LCR while failing NSFR by holding a large buffer against structurally short funding.
