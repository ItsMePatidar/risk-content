# 3.4 Liquidity Stress Testing

## Definition

Liquidity stress testing projects a lender's cash position forward under scenarios in which funding becomes unavailable, and reports the **survival horizon** - the number of days the lender can meet its obligations before counterbalancing capacity is exhausted. Unlike the LCR, which applies a prescribed 30-day stress uniformly across firms, liquidity stress testing uses the lender's own funding structure, its own contingency arrangements, and scenarios calibrated to its own vulnerabilities. The output is a date, not a ratio, which is what makes it useful to a board.

## Why it matters

The LCR passes or fails at day 30 and says nothing about day 45. For a wholesale-funded retail lender, the most likely failure path is a rating downgrade that closes the commercial paper market, followed by banks declining to roll term loans at maturity - a sequence that unfolds over six to ten weeks. Survival horizon is the metric that captures it. Equally important, stress testing tests the contingency funding plan for timing rather than for size. A securitisation pipeline that can raise ₹500 crore is worthless if it takes 45 days to execute and the buffer runs out on day 33. Almost every real liquidity failure is a timing failure that looked adequate on a static capacity table.

## Formula

```
Survival horizon = the largest T such that
    SUM over t=1 to T of Net outflow_t  <=  Counterbalancing capacity available by T

Counterbalancing capacity = HQLA x (1 - haircut)
                          + Undrawn irrevocable committed lines
                          + Securitisable pool value x (1 - haircut), available from day D
```

where:
- `Net outflow_t` = contractual outflows less stressed inflows on day t, in ₹
- `haircut` = the discount applied to realisable value under stressed market conditions, in %
- `D` = execution lead time for the funding source, in days - the parameter that most often determines the answer

Scenario types:

Idiosyncratic - the lender alone is affected. A rating downgrade, an adverse audit finding, a governance event. Markets function; they are simply closed to this borrower. Funding sources disappear, asset sales remain possible at a discount.

Market-wide - the sector is affected. Securitisation markets close, bank lines are conserved system-wide, asset haircuts widen. Everyone is selling the same assets.

Combined - an idiosyncratic event during market-wide stress. This is the scenario that must anchor the contingency funding plan, because it is the one in which every mitigation is simultaneously impaired.

Management actions modelled must be realistic and sequenced: halting new disbursements is immediate and large, drawing committed lines takes days, executing a direct assignment takes weeks, and raising equity takes months.

## Worked Example

Example: the NBFC from 3.1 is downgraded from AA- to A+ following a rating agency review of unsecured retail asset quality. Scenario assumptions:

- Commercial paper cannot be rolled. The ₹350 crore matures and is repaid.
- Bank term loans continue to amortise on schedule; no acceleration, but no new sanctions.
- Undrawn bank lines total ₹240 crore, of which ₹140 crore is revocable and assumed unavailable. Usable: ₹100 crore.
- New disbursements are halted immediately, saving ₹121 crore per month.
- Collection efficiency falls from 97.2% to 89.4% as field collections capacity is diverted and borrower sentiment turns. Inflows fall to ₹7.45 crore per day.
- A ₹620 crore home loan pool is eligible for direct assignment, at a 15% haircut in stressed conditions, realising ₹527 crore - available from day 45.

Counterbalancing capacity available from day 1: ₹556 crore HQLA plus ₹100 crore of committed lines = ₹656 crore.

Weekly cash flow projection:

| Week | Outflows | Inflows | Net outflow | Cumulative | Capacity remaining |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹118 cr | ₹52 cr | ₹66 cr | ₹66 cr | ₹590 cr |
| 2 | ₹196 cr | ₹52 cr | ₹144 cr | ₹210 cr | ₹446 cr |
| 3 | ₹210 cr | ₹52 cr | ₹158 cr | ₹368 cr | ₹288 cr |
| 4 | ₹238 cr | ₹52 cr | ₹186 cr | ₹554 cr | ₹102 cr |
| 5 | ₹180 cr | ₹52 cr | ₹128 cr | ₹682 cr | (₹26 cr) |

Capacity is exhausted during week 5:

```
Remaining at end of week 4      = ₹102 cr
Week 5 net outflow per day      = ₹128 cr / 7 = ₹18.3 cr
Days of survival into week 5    = ₹102 cr / ₹18.3 cr = 5.6 days

Survival horizon = 28 + 5.6 = approximately 33 days
```

The direct assignment arrives on day 45. The lender fails twelve days before its largest contingency source becomes available.

This is the finding that matters, and it is invisible on a static capacity table - total counterbalancing capacity of ₹656 crore plus ₹527 crore is ₹1,183 crore against 60 days of net outflow of roughly ₹1,080 crore, which looks comfortable. Sequencing destroys the comfort.

Remediation options, in order of effectiveness per rupee of cost:

Pre-arranging the direct assignment so it executes by day 25 rather than day 45 extends the survival horizon to approximately day 62, at the cost of maintaining documentation and a standing buyer relationship. Converting the ₹140 crore of revocable lines to irrevocable committed lines adds roughly 8 days, at the cost of commitment fees. Raising the HQLA buffer by ₹200 crore adds roughly 11 days, at the cost of the yield give-up between government securities and loan assets - the most expensive of the three per day purchased.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Summing counterbalancing capacity without timing. Capacity that arrives after the buffer is exhausted is not capacity. The execution lead time is as important as the amount.
- Assuming collection efficiency holds. Funding stress and credit stress arrive together, and a lender that models a funding shock with unimpaired inflows understates the problem materially.
- Counting revocable bank lines. They are withdrawn in exactly the scenario being modelled.
- Modelling asset sales at book value. Stressed haircuts on retail pools run 12-20%, and in a market-wide scenario buyers may be absent at any price.
- Treating disbursement halts as costless. Stopping origination preserves cash and destroys the franchise, breaks channel relationships, and is visible to the market within days - which can accelerate the funding withdrawal it was meant to survive.
