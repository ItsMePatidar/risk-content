# 5.3 Risk Governance and the Three Lines of Defence

## Definition

The **three lines of defence** model allocates responsibility for risk management across an institution. The **first line** is the business - it owns the risks it creates and operates the controls that manage them. The **second line** is the independent risk management and compliance function - it sets the framework, defines standards, monitors exposure, and challenges the first line. The **third line** is internal audit - it provides independent assurance to the board on whether the first and second lines are working. The board and its committees sit above all three; external audit and the supervisor sit outside.

## Why it matters

The model exists because the people who generate risk and the people who assess it must not be the same people. That sounds obvious and fails constantly in practice, usually through drift rather than through decision. A credit risk function that starts by setting policy and ends up approving individual deviations has become first line while still being described as second. An internal audit function that helps design controls cannot subsequently provide assurance over them. Both failures remove a layer of protection while leaving the organisation chart unchanged, which is why they are hard to see.

India has specific structural requirements. RBI requires NBFCs above a prescribed asset-size threshold to appoint a Chief Risk Officer with a fixed tenure, no dual-hatting into business roles, and removal only with board approval and intimation to RBI - a rule written precisely because CROs were being removed for inconvenient findings. Under Scale Based Regulation and the compliance function guidelines, larger NBFCs must also maintain an independent compliance function under a Chief Compliance Officer.

## Formula

Allocation across the retail lending lifecycle:

| Activity | First line | Second line | Third line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sourcing and channel management | Owns channel selection, agent onboarding, commission structure | Sets channel eligibility standards, monitors channel-level performance | Audits channel due diligence and commission controls |
| Underwriting | Executes decisions within policy | Owns credit policy, scorecard governance, cutoff setting | Audits policy adherence and deviation approval |
| Disbursement and servicing | Operates controls, reconciles, resolves exceptions | Sets control standards, monitors KRIs, runs RCSA challenge | Tests control operation independently |
| Collections | Executes strategy, manages agencies | Sets conduct standards, monitors complaints and agency performance | Audits agency oversight and conduct compliance |
| Models | Uses model output in decisions | Independently validates models, monitors drift | Audits the validation function itself |

Two boundary rules do most of the work. The second line must not make individual transaction decisions, because doing so removes the independent challenge over those decisions. The third line must not design or operate controls, because it cannot then provide assurance over them.

Escalation runs upward through defined committees: an executive risk committee, a board risk management committee, and an audit committee, with the CRO reporting to the board committee rather than only to the chief executive.

## Worked Example

Example: a governance failure at the NBFC, identified during a review of underwriting deviations.

Policy permits approval outside the scorecard cutoff on documented grounds. Deviation approval authority sits with a regional credit manager. The reporting line for that role runs to the regional business head, whose variable compensation is driven by disbursement volume.

Observed position over the year:

```
Accounts booked                        = 60,500
Approved on deviation                  = 8.4% = 5,082 accounts
Average ticket                         = ₹3.60 lakh
Exposure approved on deviation         = ₹182.95 cr

12-month default rate, policy-approved  = 4.10%
12-month default rate, deviation-approved = 9.43%
Excess default rate                     = 5.33 pp

Excess expected loss = 5.33% x 72% LGD x ₹182.95 cr = ₹7.02 cr per annum
```

The deviation portfolio defaults at 2.3 times the policy-approved rate, which by itself is not evidence of a problem - deviations should perform worse, or the policy is too tight. The problem is the ratio combined with the volume and the reporting line. At 8.4% of bookings, deviation approval is not an exception mechanism, it is a parallel underwriting channel, and it is operated by a role that reports to the function whose targets it relieves.

The governance diagnosis is a first-and-second-line boundary failure. The remedies are structural rather than analytical: move the deviation approval authority into an independent credit function with a reporting line to the CRO, cap deviation volume at a stated percentage of bookings with breaches escalating to the board risk committee, and report deviation performance separately to that committee every quarter.

Note what the three lines each should have caught, and when. The first line should have seen the vintage performance of its own deviation cohort. The second line should have set the deviation cap and monitored it as a KRI. The third line should have tested whether the approval authority was independent. That all three missed it is the characteristic signature of a boundary failure - each line assumed the control sat with another.

## How it connects

The second line's monitoring instruments are the RCSA of 4.5 and the KRIs of 4.6, and the deviation rate above belongs in both. The independent model validation obligation is the model risk framework of 4.7. The appetite limits of 5.2 are set by the board and enforced through this structure, and the escalation path when they breach runs through these committees. The ICAAP of 5.1 is prepared by the second line, challenged by the third, and owned by the board.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Letting the second line make transaction decisions. Once the risk function approves individual deviations, nobody independent is left to challenge them.
- Using internal audit to fix problems. Audit that designs or implements controls has forfeited its ability to assure them.
- Treating the model as an organisation chart. The lines are functions, not departments. A small lender can operate all three with a handful of people, provided the boundaries hold.
- Allowing the CRO to hold business responsibilities. The Indian rule against dual-hatting exists because the conflict is not manageable by good intentions.
- Assuming outsourced activity sits outside the model. Collections agencies and lending service providers operate within the first line, under the lender's ownership, and the second and third lines must cover them.
