# 5.2 Risk Appetite Framework

## Definition

A **risk appetite framework (RAF)** is the board-approved articulation of how much risk, and which kinds, an institution is willing to accept in pursuit of its strategy. It combines qualitative statements about risks the institution will and will not take with quantitative metrics carrying explicit limits. Three related concepts must be kept distinct: **risk capacity** is the maximum risk the institution could absorb before breaching a regulatory minimum or failing; **risk appetite** is the level the board chooses to operate at, deliberately below capacity; and **risk tolerance** is the acceptable variation around appetite before escalation is required.

## Why it matters

Without a stated appetite, risk decisions are made implicitly by whoever is closest to the transaction, and the institution discovers its actual appetite only in hindsight. The framework's value is that it forces the board to make the trade-off explicitly and in advance - a lender that says it will hold unsecured exposure below 45% of the book has decided something, and the sales function knows it before it books the business rather than after. The framework is also the connective tissue between capital, earnings, and portfolio decisions. Every limit in it should trace back to either capital capacity or earnings volatility that the board is prepared to accept, and a limit that traces to neither is a number somebody liked.

## Formula

The cascade runs top-down:

Capacity - set by regulatory minimums and by the point at which the institution's funding or franchise fails. For capital, capacity is the regulatory CRAR floor. For liquidity, it is the survival horizon at which the institution cannot meet obligations.

Appetite - the board's chosen operating level, set with enough headroom that a plausible stress does not breach capacity. The stress results from ICAAP determine how much headroom is enough.

Tolerance and triggers - an amber band that obliges escalation and a management response before the appetite limit itself is breached.

Limits - appetite allocated down to products, portfolios, channels, and geographies, so that front-line decisions are constrained by construction rather than by exhortation.

A well-formed RAF metric has five components: a definition, a capacity level, an appetite limit, an amber trigger, and a named owner accountable for the response. Metrics without an owner do not produce action.

## Worked Example

Example: risk appetite dashboard for the NBFC, drawing metrics from all four preceding modules.

| Metric | Capacity | Appetite | Amber trigger | Actual | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CRAR | 15.00% (regulatory) | >= 17.50% | 16.00% | 16.71% | Amber |
| Tier 1 ratio | 10.00% (regulatory) | >= 13.00% | 11.50% | 14.69% | Green |
| Annual credit cost | 4.80% (breakeven) | <= 3.10% | 3.50% | 2.95% | Green |
| GNPA ratio | - | <= 3.25% | 3.75% | 2.91% | Green |
| Unsecured share of book | - | <= 45.0% | 50.0% | 41.3% | Green |
| Liquidity coverage ratio | 100% (regulatory) | >= 120% | 110% | 71.7% | Red |
| Survival horizon under stress | 30 days | >= 90 days | 60 days | 33 days | Red |
| Delta EVE / Tier 1 at 200 bps | 15.00% (outlier test) | <= 10.00% | 12.00% | 12.06% | Amber |
| Largest single-state exposure | - | <= 22.0% | 25.0% | 24.6% | Amber |
| Operational loss / disbursement | - | <= 35 bps | 45 bps | 43 bps | Amber |
| Complaints per 1,000 collections accounts | - | <= 2.5 | 4.0 | 3.4 | Amber |

Reading the dashboard. Every credit metric is green - credit cost, GNPA, and unsecured concentration all sit comfortably inside appetite. Every liquidity metric is red. The binding constraint on this institution is not the risk category its risk function spends most of its time on.

The two red items are the same problem viewed twice: an LCR of 71.7% and a 33-day survival horizon both reflect ₹350 crore of commercial paper funding a book that cannot generate cash at that speed. The board response is a single action - term out the commercial paper - which from 3.3 moves the LCR to 149.1% and from 3.4 extends the survival horizon past 60 days. One decision clears both reds.

Consistency check against strategy. The stated plan grows the book 25% a year. Test that against the CRAR appetite of 17.5%:

```
RWA at end of year 1        = ₹4,220 cr x 1.25    = ₹5,275 cr
Capital required at appetite = ₹5,275 cr x 17.50% = ₹923 cr
Capital available            = ₹705 cr + ₹83 cr retained = ₹788 cr
Shortfall against appetite   = ₹135 cr
```

The growth plan is inconsistent with the capital appetite in year one. This is the single most valuable test an RAF performs, and it is arithmetic rather than judgement. Either the appetite is lowered, capital is raised, or growth is reduced - and the framework forces the board to choose explicitly rather than to discover the answer through a CRAR breach.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Setting appetite at capacity. If appetite equals the regulatory minimum, any adverse move is simultaneously an appetite breach and a regulatory breach, and the framework provides no early warning.
- Writing qualitative statements without metrics. "We have a low appetite for conduct risk" constrains nothing. A complaints-per-thousand limit with an owner does.
- Cascading appetite to the portfolio but not to the front line. A book-level unsecured concentration limit does not stop a channel from booking the wrong business unless it is translated into channel-level and product-level limits.
- Reviewing metrics individually. The dashboard above has eight amber and red items; reviewed one at a time each is arguable, and read together they describe an institution operating beyond its stated appetite in liquidity and conduct.
- Failing to test appetite against the business plan. Appetite and strategy are set in different forums and frequently contradict each other, and the contradiction surfaces as a breach a year later.
