# 2.1 Fundamentals of Market Risk for a Lending Book

## Definition

**Market risk** is the risk of loss from movements in market prices and rates - interest rates, exchange rates, equity and commodity prices. A retail lender holds almost no traded positions, so for a personal loan, credit card, home loan, or auto loan book, market risk narrows to two things: the risk that funding costs rise faster than loan yields, and the risk that assets and liabilities reprice on different dates and against different benchmarks. Everything else in the market risk taxonomy is either immaterial to a retail lender or confined to a small treasury portfolio.

## Why it matters

Retail lending runs on a spread, and that spread is where rate risk lands. A lender earning 15.2% on assets and paying 8.65% for funding has a 655 bps cushion that looks generous until you notice how little of it is contractually locked. Fixed-rate personal loans cannot reprice at all during their life, while the borrowings that fund them roll over every one to three years. If refinancing spreads widen, the loss is immediate and unrecoverable - the lender cannot go back to existing borrowers and raise their rate. This is why NBFC business models are more rate-exposed than bank models: banks fund with sticky low-cost deposits, NBFCs fund with wholesale borrowings that reprice on schedule regardless of what the loan book is doing.

## Formula

Net interest income is the quantity at risk:

```
NII = SUM(A_i x y_i) - SUM(L_j x c_j)
```

where:
- `A_i` = balance of asset class i, in ₹
- `y_i` = yield on asset class i, in % per annum
- `L_j` = balance of funding source j, in ₹
- `c_j` = cost of funding source j, in % per annum

Rate risk in a lending book decomposes into four channels:

Repricing (gap) risk - assets and liabilities reset at different times. A floating-rate home loan resetting quarterly against a term loan resetting annually creates a timing mismatch even when both are floating.

Basis risk - assets and liabilities are floating but linked to different benchmarks. A repo-linked home loan and an MCLR-linked bank borrowing both move with rates, but not by the same amount at the same time.

Yield curve risk - the curve changes shape rather than shifting in parallel. A steepener hurts a lender funding long assets with short money differently from a parallel shift of the same average size.

Optionality risk - borrowers exercise embedded options. Prepayment and balance transfer are the retail versions, and they are asymmetric: borrowers refinance away when rates fall, and stay when rates rise.

The refinancing exposure over a horizon:

```
Refinancing cost impact = B_maturing x Delta_spread
```

where:
- `B_maturing` = borrowings maturing and requiring refinancing within the horizon, in ₹
- `Delta_spread` = change in the credit spread the lender must pay over the benchmark, in %

## Worked Example

Example: an NBFC with a ₹4,450 crore retail book - ₹1,840 crore of fixed-rate personal loans yielding 17.60% and ₹2,610 crore of repo-linked home loans yielding 9.50%, for a blended asset yield of 12.85%. Funding is ₹4,010 crore at a blended 8.65%, giving a gross spread of 420 bps.

Over the next twelve months, ₹1,530 crore of borrowings mature and must be refinanced. Sector sentiment toward unsecured retail deteriorates and the NBFC's borrowing spread widens by 65 bps.

```
Refinancing cost impact = ₹1,530 cr x 0.65% = ₹9.95 cr per annum
As a share of the asset book = ₹9.95 cr / ₹4,450 cr = 22.4 bps
```

The blended spread compresses from 420 bps to 398 bps. Now consider the recovery options:

The ₹2,610 crore home loan book is repo-linked, so it reprices only if the policy rate moves - a widening of the NBFC's own credit spread does not lift the loan rate at all. The ₹1,840 crore personal loan book is fixed-rate, so existing accounts cannot be repriced under any circumstance. Only new personal loan originations can carry a higher rate, and at a 23-month behavioural life roughly half the book turns over in a year. Repricing new business by 45 bps recovers ₹4.1 crore in the first year, leaving ₹5.85 crore of the loss to be absorbed.

This is the defining feature of market risk in a retail book: the loss arrives on the liability side within weeks, and the offsetting action on the asset side takes years to work through.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Treating market risk as a treasury problem. For a retail lender the exposure sits in the funding structure of the loan book itself, not in a trading desk.
- Assuming floating-rate assets eliminate rate risk. They eliminate contractual repricing lag, not basis risk, not timing mismatch against liabilities, and not the credit consequences of higher EMIs.
- Ignoring the asymmetry of prepayment. Borrowers refinance out of high-rate fixed loans when rates fall, so the lender keeps the bad half of the book in both directions.
- Confusing spread risk with rate risk. A policy rate move affects both sides of the balance sheet; a widening of the lender's own credit spread affects only the liability side and has no natural hedge.
