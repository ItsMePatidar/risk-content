# 2.2 Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB)

## Definition

**IRRBB** is the risk to a lender's earnings and economic value from adverse interest rate movements in positions held in the banking book - that is, the loan book and the funding that supports it, held at accrual rather than fair value. It is measured from two perspectives. The **earnings perspective** asks how net interest income changes over the next one to three years, and is captured by **Delta NII**. The **economic value perspective** asks how the present value of all future banking book cash flows changes, and is captured by **Delta EVE** (economic value of equity). The two can move in opposite directions on the same shock, and both are required.

## Why it matters

IRRBB is a Pillar 2 risk - it carries no standard Pillar 1 capital charge, but supervisors assess it and can impose capital add-ons. RBI's IRRBB guidelines require lenders to compute Delta EVE and Delta NII under six prescribed shock scenarios, with an outlier test triggered when Delta EVE exceeds 15% of Tier 1 capital. The practical importance is that the two metrics disagree routinely. A lender can report improving NII from a rate rise while its economic value falls sharply, because NII looks twelve months ahead and EVE looks at the whole book. Managing to the earnings number alone is how lenders accumulate long-dated value exposure that never appears in quarterly reporting.

## Formula

Repricing gap analysis places every asset and liability into a time bucket according to its next repricing date - not its maturity.

```
Gap_t = RSA_t - RSL_t

Delta_NII = SUM over t of [ Gap_t x Delta_r x (T - m_t) / 12 ]
```

where:
- `RSA_t` = rate-sensitive assets repricing in bucket t, in ₹
- `RSL_t` = rate-sensitive liabilities repricing in bucket t, in ₹
- `Gap_t` = repricing gap for bucket t, in ₹; positive means asset-sensitive
- `Delta_r` = the rate shock, in % per annum
- `T` = the earnings horizon, in months, normally 12
- `m_t` = midpoint of bucket t in months, so `(T - m_t)/12` is the fraction of the horizon over which the repriced position earns the new rate

The economic value measure uses duration:

```
DGAP = D_A - D_L x (L / A)

Delta_EVE = - DGAP x A x Delta_r
```

where:
- `D_A` = modified duration of assets, in years
- `D_L` = modified duration of liabilities, in years
- `A` = total assets, in ₹
- `L` = total liabilities, in ₹
- `DGAP` = duration gap, in years; positive means economic value falls when rates rise

For floating-rate retail assets, duration is measured to the next reset date, not to final maturity. A 20-year repo-linked home loan resetting quarterly has a duration of roughly 0.125 years, not 20.

The six RBI/Basel shock scenarios are: parallel up, parallel down, steepener, flattener, short rate up, and short rate down. Delta EVE is assessed against the worst of the six; Delta NII conventionally against the two parallel shocks.

## Worked Example

Example: the ₹4,450 crore NBFC book from 2.1, bucketed by next repricing date, with a +100 bps parallel shock and a 12-month earnings horizon.

| Bucket | Midpoint | RSA (₹ cr) | RSL (₹ cr) | Gap (₹ cr) | Months earning new rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1-30 days | 0.5 mo | 210 | 350 | (140) | 11.5 |
| 31-90 days | 2.0 mo | 900 | 520 | 380 | 10.0 |
| 91-180 days | 4.5 mo | 780 | 610 | 170 | 7.5 |
| 181-365 days | 9.0 mo | 720 | 1,325 | (605) | 3.0 |
| Total 12 months | | 2,610 | 2,805 | (195) | |

Earnings impact:

```
1-30 days:   (140) x 1.00% x 11.5/12 = (₹1.342 cr)
31-90 days:    380 x 1.00% x 10.0/12 =  ₹3.167 cr
91-180 days:   170 x 1.00% x  7.5/12 =  ₹1.063 cr
181-365 days:(605) x 1.00% x  3.0/12 = (₹1.513 cr)
                                        -----------
Delta NII                             =  ₹1.375 cr
```

The cumulative twelve-month gap is negative at minus ₹195 crore, which would suggest a rise in rates hurts earnings. It does not. The positive gaps sit in early buckets where they earn the new rate for most of the year, while the large negative gap sits at nine months and only bites for three. Cumulative gap sign is not a reliable indicator of earnings direction - the time weighting is what determines it.

Economic value impact. Asset duration is 1.85 years (long home loans, but repricing quarterly, blended with fixed-rate personal loans averaging under a year of remaining duration). Liability duration is 1.12 years. Liabilities are ₹4,010 crore against assets of ₹4,450 crore.

```
DGAP = 1.85 - 1.12 x (4,010 / 4,450) = 1.85 - 1.009 = 0.841 years

Delta EVE = -0.841 x ₹4,450 cr x 1.00% = (₹37.41 cr)
```

Against Tier 1 capital of ₹620 crore, that is 6.03% - inside the 15% outlier threshold, but the direction is the point. The same +100 bps shock adds ₹1.38 crore to next year's earnings and destroys ₹37.41 crore of economic value. The lender is short duration in the earnings window and long duration beyond it.

Optionality overlay. If rates instead fall 100 bps, the model shows a symmetric EVE gain of ₹37.41 crore. It will not materialise. Home loan borrowers respond to falling rates with balance transfers, and the lender loses its highest-quality accounts to competitors at exactly the moment the modelled value gain appears. Standard practice is to apply a prepayment overlay - assuming, for example, that 18% of the home loan book prepays within twelve months of a 100 bps fall against 6% in the base case - which cuts the modelled EVE gain by roughly a third.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Bucketing by maturity rather than by next repricing date. A 20-year home loan resetting quarterly belongs in the 91-180 day bucket, not the over-5-year bucket. Getting this wrong inverts the entire gap profile.
- Reading cumulative gap as the answer. As the worked example shows, a negative twelve-month gap produced positive Delta NII because of time weighting within the horizon.
- Managing to Delta NII alone. It is the metric that appears in quarterly results, which is exactly why economic value exposure accumulates unnoticed behind it.
- Modelling rate falls symmetrically with rate rises. Prepayment optionality makes the retail book's response asymmetric, and ignoring it overstates the benefit of falling rates.
- Treating repo-linked assets and MCLR-linked liabilities as a matched floating position. They are both floating and still mismatched, because the benchmarks move by different amounts on different dates. This is basis risk, and gap tables built on a single yield curve do not capture it.
