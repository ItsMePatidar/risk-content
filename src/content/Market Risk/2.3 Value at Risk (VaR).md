# 2.3 Value at Risk (VaR)

## Definition

**Value at Risk** estimates the maximum loss on a portfolio over a specified holding period at a specified confidence level, under normal market conditions. A one-day 99% VaR of ₹4 crore means that on 99 days out of 100, the loss should not exceed ₹4 crore. VaR is a trading book measure built for fair-valued positions, and its direct application to a retail lending book is limited - a personal loan portfolio held at amortised cost has no daily mark-to-market, and its loss distribution is a credit distribution described by the models in Module 1, not a market one.

For a retail lender, VaR is relevant in three specific places: the treasury portfolio of government securities held for liquidity, loan pools warehoused at fair value pending securitisation or direct assignment, and unhedged foreign currency borrowings. This topic is kept introductory because those exposures are usually a small fraction of a retail lender's balance sheet.

## Why it matters

Where a retail lender does hold fair-valued positions, they carry mark-to-market risk that the accrual-accounted loan book does not. A securitisation warehouse is the clearest case: the lender originates a pool over several weeks, holds it at fair value, and sells it. During the warehousing window, a rise in yields reduces the pool's realisable value and shrinks the gain on sale - a real P&L event unconnected to the credit quality of the loans. VaR is the standard way to size that exposure and to set warehouse limits. It also matters because supervisors and boards use VaR language, so a retail risk practitioner needs to read the number correctly even when they do not produce it.

## Formula

Three estimation methods are in general use.

Parametric (variance-covariance) - assumes returns are normally distributed and computes VaR from volatility:

```
VaR = z_alpha x sigma x sqrt(h) x V
```

where:
- `z_alpha` = the standard normal quantile at the chosen confidence level; 1.645 at 95%, 2.326 at 99%
- `sigma` = standard deviation of daily returns, in %
- `h` = holding period, in days
- `sqrt(h)` = the square-root-of-time scaling factor, valid only if daily returns are independent
- `V` = portfolio market value, in ₹

For an interest-rate-sensitive position, return volatility is derived from yield volatility through duration:

```
sigma_value = MD x sigma_yield

VaR = z_alpha x MD x sigma_yield x sqrt(h) x V
```

where `MD` is modified duration in years and `sigma_yield` is the daily standard deviation of yield changes, in decimal form.

Historical simulation - revalues the current portfolio under each of the last 250 or 500 daily market moves, ranks the resulting P&Ls, and reads off the relevant percentile. It makes no distributional assumption and captures observed fat tails, but cannot produce a scenario worse than anything in the sample window.

Monte Carlo - simulates a large number of paths from an assumed process. Flexible, computationally heavy, and only as good as the assumed correlations.

Expected shortfall (ES), the average loss conditional on breaching VaR, addresses VaR's central weakness of saying nothing about tail severity:

```
ES_alpha = E[ Loss | Loss > VaR_alpha ]
```

Basel's revised market risk framework replaced 99% VaR with 97.5% expected shortfall for exactly this reason.

## Worked Example

Example: an auto loan pool of ₹186 crore being warehoused ahead of a securitisation, carried at fair value. Modified duration is 1.72 years. Daily volatility of the relevant benchmark yield is 4.8 bps. The lender expects to hold the pool for ten trading days before sale, and reports at 99% confidence.

```
VaR = 2.326 x 1.72 x 0.00048 x sqrt(10) x ₹186 cr

Step 1: 2.326 x 1.72              = 4.001
Step 2: 4.001 x 0.00048           = 0.001920
Step 3: 0.001920 x 3.1623         = 0.006073
Step 4: 0.006073 x ₹186 cr        = ₹1.13 cr
```

The ten-day 99% VaR is ₹1.13 crore, or 0.61% of pool value. Read correctly: in roughly one ten-day warehousing window out of a hundred, the mark-to-market loss should exceed ₹1.13 crore. If the expected gain on sale is ₹5.2 crore, a ₹1.13 crore tail move consumes 22% of it, which is the number that should drive the warehouse size limit and the decision on whether to hedge with interest rate futures.

Expected shortfall on the same position, under a normal assumption, is approximately 1.15 times the 99% VaR, or ₹1.30 crore. The gap widens considerably under historical simulation on a sample that includes a policy surprise, which is the practical argument for not relying on the parametric number alone.

## How it connects

VaR measures value change on fair-valued positions; Delta EVE in 2.2 measures value change on accrual-book positions under a deterministic shock. They answer similar questions with different machinery, and a retail lender needs both because its balance sheet contains both kinds of position. The parametric VaR above underlies the internal models approach in the Basel market risk framework (2.4), where the standardized alternative is described. The warehousing exposure itself connects to credit risk, since the pool being marked is a retail loan pool whose credit quality was set by the underwriting standards of 1.7.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Applying VaR to the accrual loan book. A held-to-maturity personal loan portfolio has no daily price. Its loss distribution is the credit loss distribution from Module 1, and running VaR on it produces a number with no economic meaning.
- Reading VaR as a maximum loss. It is a threshold, not a ceiling. A 99% VaR is expected to be breached roughly two to three times a year, and says nothing about how far.
- Scaling one-day VaR by the square root of time without checking the assumption. Square-root scaling requires independent daily returns. In trending or stressed markets, returns autocorrelate and the scaled figure understates risk.
- Assuming normality on positions with optionality. Prepayment-embedded loan pools have negatively convex payoffs, and a delta-only parametric VaR misses the convexity entirely.
- Treating a low VaR as evidence of low risk. VaR is calibrated to normal conditions by construction. It is silent on the scenarios that stress testing (2.5) exists to address.
