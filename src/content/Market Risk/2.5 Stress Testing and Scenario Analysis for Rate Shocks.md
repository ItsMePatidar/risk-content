# 2.5 Stress Testing and Scenario Analysis for Rate Shocks

## Definition

Rate-shock stress testing projects the impact of severe but plausible interest rate movements on a lender's earnings, economic value, and capital. Where VaR asks what happens on a bad day under normal conditions, stress testing asks what happens in conditions that are not normal. For a retail lender the exercise has a feature that trading-book stress testing does not: a rate shock does not only reprice the balance sheet, it changes borrower behaviour and credit quality. The full impact runs through three channels simultaneously - margin, valuation, and credit.

## Why it matters

Managing the three channels separately understates the total. A treasury team models the gap impact, a credit team models the PD impact, and nobody adds them up. For a retail lender the credit channel is usually the largest of the three by a wide margin, which inverts the intuition carried over from trading businesses. Supervisors expect the combined view: RBI's IRRBB framework prescribes six rate scenarios and an outlier test against Tier 1 capital, and ICAAP requires the capital consequences of integrated stress scenarios rather than of isolated risk factors.

## Formula

The prescribed IRRBB shock scenarios:

| Scenario | Description |
|---|---|
| Parallel up | Entire curve shifts up |
| Parallel down | Entire curve shifts down |
| Steepener | Short rates fall, long rates rise |
| Flattener | Short rates rise, long rates fall |
| Short rate up | Short end rises, long end unchanged |
| Short rate down | Short end falls, long end unchanged |

Delta EVE is assessed against the worst of all six. The outlier test:

```
Outlier if: |Delta EVE_worst| / Tier 1 capital > 15%
```

The integrated retail impact is the sum of three channels:

```
Total earnings impact = Delta NII_gap + Refinancing impact + Delta Credit cost

Delta Credit cost = (EL_stressed - EL_base) x Portfolio exposure
```

where `Delta NII_gap` comes from the repricing gap (2.2), `Refinancing impact` comes from spread widening on maturing borrowings (2.1), and `Delta Credit cost` comes from the macro-to-PD model (1.14). The credit channel operates through two distinct retail mechanisms: higher EMIs on floating-rate loans raise FOIR and therefore PD, and higher rates on new lending raise the debt burden of the incoming book.

Reverse stress testing inverts the question - instead of asking what a 250 bps shock does to capital, it asks what shock would exhaust the capital buffer. This is often more informative for a board than a scenario-driven number.

## Worked Example

Example: a +250 bps parallel shock applied to the ₹4,450 crore NBFC book, with Tier 1 capital of ₹620 crore.

Channel 1 - earnings from repricing. Scaling the Delta NII of ₹1.375 crore per 100 bps from 2.2:

```
Delta NII = ₹1.375 cr x 2.5 = +₹3.44 cr
```

Channel 2 - refinancing. The ₹1,530 crore of maturing borrowings reprice to the higher benchmark, which the gap calculation already captures. On top of that, sector stress widens the NBFC's own credit spread by 65 bps as in 2.1:

```
Spread impact = ₹1,530 cr x 0.65% = (₹9.95 cr)
```

Channel 3 - credit. Using the econometric sensitivity from 1.14, with a lending rate change of +2.5 percentage points and unemployment held flat:

```
logit(DR) = -3.1520 + (0.110 x 2.5) = -2.8770
PD_stressed = 1 / (1 + e^2.8770) = 5.33%
EL_stressed = 5.33% x 72% = 3.84%
EL_base                    = 2.95%
Delta EL                   = 0.89 pp

Delta Credit cost = 0.89% x ₹1,840 cr = (₹16.38 cr)
```

Combined earnings impact:

```
+₹3.44 cr - ₹9.95 cr - ₹16.38 cr = (₹22.89 cr)
```

The credit channel is 72% of the total loss and the pure market risk channels together are 28%. A rate stress test run by a treasury function in isolation would have reported a gain of ₹3.44 crore.

Economic value and the outlier test:

```
Delta EVE = -0.841 x ₹4,450 cr x 2.50% = (₹93.53 cr)
Delta EVE / Tier 1 = ₹93.53 cr / ₹620 cr = 15.09%
```

The book breaches the 15% outlier threshold at a 250 bps parallel shock, marginally. That triggers supervisory dialogue and, in practice, either a duration reduction on the asset side or lengthening of liability duration.

Home loan borrower channel, which drives the credit number above. Taking the ₹42,00,000 / 20-year / 8.60% loan from 1.14 at +250 bps:

```
EMI at 8.60%  = ₹36,715
EMI at 11.10% = ₹43,608
Increase      = ₹6,893, or 18.8%
```

A borrower originated at a 42.0% FOIR moves to 49.9% with no change in income. Where the lender responds by extending tenor instead of raising the EMI, the FOIR stays at 42.0% and the stress becomes invisible to every monitoring metric - while the loan term extends past the borrower's expected retirement. Both responses must be modelled, because they produce identical near-term reported metrics and very different long-term risk.

## How it connects

This topic assembles the gap analysis of 2.2, the refinancing exposure of 2.1, and the macro-to-PD model of 1.14 into one number. The scenarios are shared with IFRS9's forward-looking overlay (1.11), where the same rate path feeds probability-weighted ECL. The capital consequence connects to the RWA structure of 2.4 and 1.10. Where the credit channel dominates, as it does here, the mitigations are credit mitigations - tightening FOIR caps at origination (1.7) and reweighting collections capacity (1.12) - rather than treasury hedges.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Running rate stress and credit stress as separate exercises. For a retail lender they are the same scenario, and the credit channel usually dominates.
- Applying only parallel shocks. A flattener can be far more damaging than a parallel move of similar magnitude for a lender funding long assets with short money, which is why six scenarios are prescribed rather than two.
- Scaling linearly from a 100 bps result. Delta NII scales close to linearly because the gap structure is fixed, but the credit channel does not - the logit form is convex, so a 250 bps shock produces more than 2.5 times the credit loss of a 100 bps shock.
- Assuming management action without modelling its cost. Cutting duration or hedging is possible, but both consume margin, and a stress test that assumes costless mitigation is not a stress test.
- Ignoring tenor extension as a response to EMI shock. It is the standard Indian practice on floating-rate retail loans and it removes the stress from FOIR reporting without removing it from the borrower.
