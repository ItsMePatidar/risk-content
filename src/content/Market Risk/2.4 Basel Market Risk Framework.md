# 2.4 Basel Market Risk Framework

## Definition

The Basel market risk framework sets regulatory capital for positions in the **trading book** - instruments held with intent to trade or to hedge trading positions, marked to market daily. Positions in the **banking book**, which for a retail lender means essentially the entire loan portfolio and its funding, are outside this framework and are capitalised for credit risk instead. The framework offers a **Standardized Approach (SA)**, which is mandatory for all banks to compute and serves as a floor, and an **Internal Models Approach (IMA)** available with supervisory approval.

## Why it matters

For most retail lenders this framework governs a small corner of the balance sheet - the treasury's liquidity portfolio of government securities and any foreign currency exposure. The reason to understand it anyway is the boundary. Whether a position sits in the trading book or the banking book determines which capital regime applies, and the classification is not optional or reversible at will. Supervisors police the boundary precisely because arbitraging it - parking illiquid positions wherever the capital charge is lower - was a documented failure of the pre-2008 regime. The second reason is proportionality: knowing that market risk capital is typically a low single-digit percentage of a retail lender's total RWA prevents disproportionate attention being paid to it.

## Formula

Structure of the revised Standardized Approach:

Sensitivities-based method - capital is computed from the portfolio's sensitivity to prescribed risk factors, aggregated across three components. Delta captures linear sensitivity, vega captures sensitivity to volatility, and curvature captures non-linear response to large moves. These are computed within seven risk classes, of which only two are material to a retail lender: general interest rate risk (GIRR) and foreign exchange.

Default risk charge - covers jump-to-default risk on trading book credit positions.

Residual risk add-on - a flat charge for exotic and hard-to-model features.

Many Indian banks continue to compute market risk capital under the standardized duration method, in which the general market risk charge on a debt portfolio is:

```
Capital charge = SUM over bands of [ MV_band x MD_band x Assumed_yield_change_band ]

RWA equivalent = Capital charge / 0.09
```

where:
- `MV_band` = market value of positions in the time band, in ₹
- `MD_band` = modified duration of those positions, in years
- `Assumed_yield_change_band` = the supervisory yield shock for that time band, in %
- `0.09` = the minimum capital ratio used to gross a capital charge up to a risk-weighted asset equivalent

A specific risk charge is added on top, at 0% for central government securities and rising with issuer credit quality.

RBI has announced that revised Basel III capital adequacy norms for commercial banks take effect from 1 April 2027, alongside the ECL framework. The market risk component should be checked against the current RBI master direction rather than assumed stable.

## Worked Example

Example: the NBFC from 2.1 holds a ₹340 crore portfolio of central government securities in its trading book for liquidity purposes, with a modified duration of 4.6 years, against a supervisory assumed yield change of 0.75% for that time band.

```
General market risk charge = ₹340 cr x 4.6 x 0.75% = ₹11.73 cr
Specific risk charge (central government) = 0%
Total market risk capital = ₹11.73 cr
RWA equivalent = ₹11.73 cr / 0.09 = ₹130.3 cr
```

Set that against the credit risk position. From 1.10, the ₹1,840 crore personal loan book alone carries ₹2,300 crore of RWA at a 125% risk weight. Adding the ₹2,610 crore home loan book at an average 50% risk weight contributes a further ₹1,305 crore.

| Risk type | RWA | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Credit risk - personal loans | ₹2,300 cr | 61.5% |
| Credit risk - home loans | ₹1,305 cr | 34.9% |
| Market risk - trading book | ₹130 cr | 3.5% |
| Total | ₹3,735 cr | 100% |

Market risk is 3.5% of risk-weighted assets. This proportion is typical for a retail lender and is the honest answer to how much of the risk framework's attention it deserves. The same calculation at a universal bank with a substantial trading operation would put market risk at 10-20% of RWA.

## How it connects

The banking book positions excluded from this framework are governed by IRRBB (2.2), which sits in Pillar 2 with no standard capital charge - a structural point that is frequently confused. The internal models route within this framework rests on the VaR and expected shortfall machinery of 2.3. The credit risk RWA used in the comparison above comes directly from 1.10, and the combined capital impact of a rate shock across both frameworks is developed in 2.5.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Assuming IRRBB is covered by market risk capital. It is not. IRRBB is a Pillar 2 risk assessed through supervisory review, while Pillar 1 market risk capital applies only to trading book positions. A retail lender with severe banking book rate exposure and no trading book can have a market risk capital charge of zero.
- Treating the trading book boundary as an accounting choice. Classification is governed by trading intent and is subject to supervisory challenge, with restrictions on reclassification once made.
- Scaling up trading book concepts to the loan portfolio. The framework's machinery - delta, vega, curvature - is built for instruments with observable prices and does not transfer to an accrual loan book.
- Overlooking foreign currency exposure. An NBFC with external commercial borrowings carries FX risk that falls within this framework whether or not it has a trading desk, and the hedge must be assessed, not assumed.
