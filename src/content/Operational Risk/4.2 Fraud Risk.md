# 4.2 Fraud Risk

## Definition

Fraud in retail lending divides along two axes. **Third-party fraud** uses a real person's identity without their knowledge - stolen or synthetic identities, impersonation at onboarding, account takeover. **First-party fraud** is committed by the genuine applicant using their own identity, with no intention to repay from the outset; it includes income and employment misrepresentation and the credit card "bust-out", where a customer builds a clean payment record, obtains limit increases, draws the full limit and disappears. Cutting the same population differently, **application fraud** occurs at origination through falsified documents or data, while transaction fraud occurs during the life of the account.

## Why it matters

First-party fraud is the harder problem because it is indistinguishable from credit risk at the moment of decision and often for months afterwards. A first-party fraudster passes identity verification because the identity is real, passes bureau checks because the file is clean, and defaults like a bad credit customer - just faster and with a recovery rate near zero. The consequence is systematic: fraud losses land in the credit write-off line, the credit team observes that its scorecard is underperforming, and the response is to tighten the cutoff. That declines genuine borrowers while leaving the fraud channel entirely intact, because fraud does not respond to score cutoffs. Correct classification is not an accounting nicety; it determines whether the right control is applied.

## Formula

Core metrics:

```
Fraud rate = Confirmed fraud accounts / Accounts booked

FPD rate = Accounts missing the first EMI / Accounts booked

Detection rate = Fraud caught pre-disbursement / (Fraud caught + Fraud booked)

False positive ratio = Genuine applications flagged / Fraud applications flagged
```

**First payment default (FPD)** is the workhorse leading indicator. A genuine borrower who intended to repay almost never misses the very first instalment; a portfolio's FPD rate is therefore a far better proxy for fraud than its eventual default rate, and it is observable within 30 to 45 days of booking rather than 12 months.

Detection layers, in the order they fire:

Device and digital signals - device fingerprint reuse across applications, IP clustering, session behaviour such as copy-paste into income fields, GPS mismatch with declared address.

Velocity and consortium checks - the same mobile number, email, bank account, or employer appearing across multiple applications in a short window; bureau enquiry bursts indicating simultaneous applications to several lenders.

Document and data integrity - metadata analysis on uploaded payslips and bank statements, salary credit pattern verification, employer existence checks.

Network analysis - linking applications through shared attributes to expose rings, which is the only technique that reliably catches organised first-party fraud sourced through a single channel.

## Worked Example

Example: a DSA-sourced fraud ring detected through FPD monitoring at the NBFC.

Portfolio baseline: 2,520 personal loan bookings per month, portfolio FPD rate of 0.34%, average ticket ₹4,80,000.

DSA partner "Channel A" is reviewed after routine FPD monitoring:

```
Bookings sourced by Channel A over 3 months = 558
FPD accounts from Channel A                  =  23
Channel A FPD rate = 23 / 558 = 4.12%
Portfolio FPD rate                           = 0.34%
Ratio = 12.1x
```

Loss quantification. Fraud accounts recover almost nothing - no traceable borrower, no enforceable collateral, and settlement is not available against someone who does not exist or does not respond.

```
Exposure = 23 accounts x ₹4,80,000 = ₹1.10 cr
LGD on confirmed fraud = 96%
Loss = ₹1.10 cr x 96% = ₹1.06 cr
```

Investigation shows fourteen of the twenty-three shared a single employer that has no verifiable premises, and eight applications were submitted from the same device fingerprint. This is a ring, not a run of bad luck, and the correct response is channel termination and commission clawback - not a score cutoff change.

Control cost-benefit. Adding physical employer verification at ₹340 per application:

```
Applied universally:
  Cost = 7,410 apps/month x 12 x ₹340   = ₹3.02 cr per year
  Assumed fraud detection uplift = 45%
  Loss avoided = ₹9.41 cr x 45%         = ₹4.23 cr
  Benefit-to-cost ratio = 1.4x

Applied to the highest-risk 18% of applications, selected by device,
velocity, and channel signals:
  Cost = 1,334 apps/month x 12 x ₹340   = ₹0.54 cr per year
  Assumed fraud detection uplift = 38%
  Loss avoided = ₹9.41 cr x 38%         = ₹3.58 cr
  Benefit-to-cost ratio = 6.6x
```

Targeting recovers 84% of the universal control's benefit at 18% of its cost. This is the same risk-tiering logic that governed collections allocation in 1.12, and it applies to every operational control with a per-unit cost.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Treating fraud as a credit problem. Tightening the score cutoff does not reduce first-party fraud, because fraudsters present clean files by construction. It only reduces approvals of genuine marginal customers.
- Measuring fraud only on confirmed cases. Confirmation requires investigation capacity, so confirmed fraud counts what was investigated, not what occurred. FPD rate is the less biased measure.
- Optimising detection rate in isolation. A rule that catches 90% of fraud while flagging 4% of genuine applications for manual review may cost more in review capacity and abandoned applications than the fraud it prevents.
- Assuming identity verification solves first-party fraud. The identity is genuine. Verification confirms who the person is, not whether they intend to pay.
- Failing to close the channel. Ring fraud concentrates in sourcing channels. Detecting individual applications without acting on the channel means the ring simply resubmits.
