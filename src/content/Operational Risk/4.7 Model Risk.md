# 4.7 Model Risk

## Definition

**Model risk** is the risk of adverse consequences from decisions based on models that are incorrect, misapplied, or used outside their intended scope. It has three sources: the data a model was built on, the methodology it uses, and the way it is used in practice. A retail lender runs a portfolio of models rather than one - application and behavioural scorecards, ECL models, fraud detection models, collections propensity models, pricing and limit-assignment models, and the macroeconomic overlays feeding stress testing. Each carries its own risk, and the aggregate is governed through a model risk management framework covering inventory, tiering, independent validation, ongoing monitoring, and change control.

## Why it matters

Retail lending decisions are made by models, at volume, without human review. An application scorecard that has drifted out of calibration approves thousands of accounts a month at the wrong price before anyone notices, and the evidence of the error arrives twelve months later in a vintage curve. Because the feedback loop is long, model degradation must be detected through monitoring statistics rather than through outcomes. Model risk is also where the operational and credit disciplines meet: the failure is operational - a governance and monitoring failure - but the loss is booked as credit.

## Formula

Governance components:

Model inventory - every model in production, with an owner, a purpose, a development date, and a validation status. Models built in spreadsheets by the business are the usual gap.

Tiering - models ranked by materiality, with validation depth and review frequency scaled accordingly. An application scorecard driving ₹1,450 crore of annual origination is tier 1; a branch-level capacity planning tool is not.

Independent validation - performed by a function separate from development, covering conceptual soundness, data quality, implementation testing, outcome analysis, and benchmarking against a challenger.

Ongoing monitoring - the statistics below, reported at a defined frequency with thresholds and escalation.

Change control - no silent recalibration. Changes to weights, cutoffs, or scaling require documented approval and re-validation.

Monitoring statistics:

```
PSI = SUM over bands of [ (Actual% - Expected%) x ln(Actual% / Expected%) ]
```

where:
- `Expected%` = share of the development sample in the score band
- `Actual%` = share of the current scored population in the same band

Conventional interpretation: below 0.10 stable, 0.10 to 0.25 material shift requiring investigation, above 0.25 significant shift requiring action. The same formula applied to a single input variable rather than the score is the characteristic stability index (CSI), which localises where a shift originated.

Discrimination and calibration are monitored separately:

```
Discrimination: Gini and KS on the most recent closed performance window
Calibration:    Predicted PD vs observed default rate, by score band
                Calibration ratio = Observed default rate / Predicted PD
```

A model can fail either test independently, and the remedies differ - a discrimination failure requires redevelopment, a calibration failure requires an intercept adjustment.

## Worked Example

Example: quarterly monitoring of the personal loan application scorecard from 1.2 and 1.6.

Population stability:

| Score band | Development (Expected) | Current (Actual) | PSI contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below 560 | 8.0% | 17.5% | 0.0744 |
| 560-599 | 14.0% | 21.0% | 0.0284 |
| 600-639 | 22.0% | 25.0% | 0.0038 |
| 640-679 | 26.0% | 20.0% | 0.0157 |
| 680-719 | 18.0% | 11.0% | 0.0345 |
| 720 and above | 12.0% | 5.5% | 0.0507 |
| Total | 100% | 100% | **0.2075** |

Worked line for the lowest band:

```
(0.175 - 0.080) x ln(0.175 / 0.080)
  = 0.095 x ln(2.1875)
  = 0.095 x 0.78276 = 0.0744
```

PSI of 0.208 sits in the material-shift band. The population being scored has moved substantially down-score since development.

Discrimination and calibration on the same period:

```
Gini: 0.44 at development -> 0.39 currently
Predicted average PD: 4.10%
Observed 12-month default rate on the matched cohort: 5.62%
Calibration ratio = 5.62% / 4.10% = 1.37
```

Diagnosis. The Gini of 0.39 is a modest decline and remains within acceptable range for an application scorecard, so the model still rank-orders. The calibration ratio of 1.37 means it is systematically under-predicting default by 37%. Running CSI on individual characteristics localises the shift: the channel variable contributes 0.089 of the 0.208 total, consistent with the DSA share moving from 18% to 35% documented in 1.8.

The conclusion is that the model has not broken. The population has moved, and the model was calibrated to a population that no longer applies. The correct remedy is the intercept adjustment from 1.2 rather than a rebuild:

```
B0_adjusted = B0 + ln(0.0562 / 0.9438) - ln(0.0410 / 0.9590)
            = -3.62 + (-2.8232) - (-3.1520)
            = -3.2912
```

Rebuilding instead would take four to six months, during which the mis-calibrated model would continue to approve at the wrong price. Recalibration takes weeks. Distinguishing the two cases is the practical output of the monitoring statistics.

Two further checks belong in the same pack. Override rate - the share of decisions taken against the model recommendation - should be reported with the performance of overridden accounts; if overrides outperform the model, the model is missing information the underwriters have. And back-testing of the ECL model against realised losses, since an under-calibrated PD flows straight into under-provisioning under 1.11.

## How it connects

The scorecard monitored here is the model built in 1.2 and 1.6, the population shift is the channel mix change identified in 1.8, and the recalibration is the central tendency adjustment from 1.2. Under-calibrated PD propagates into expected loss (1.5), pricing (1.7), ECL provisioning (1.11), and stress testing (1.14), which is why model monitoring is a control with credit consequences across the whole framework. Model risks are surfaced and rated in RCSA (4.5), and monitoring statistics such as PSI and calibration ratio are natural KRIs (4.6).

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Reading a low PSI as evidence the model is fine. PSI measures population stability, not model performance. A stable population scored by a mis-calibrated model produces a low PSI and rising losses.
- Confusing discrimination failure with calibration failure. The first needs redevelopment, the second needs an intercept shift, and treating a calibration problem as a rebuild wastes months during which the wrong decisions continue.
- Excluding models built outside the modelling team. Spreadsheet-based limit rules, pricing grids, and collections allocation logic are models, carry model risk, and are routinely absent from the inventory.
- Validating at build and never again. Validation is not a launch gate. Tier 1 models need annual independent review and continuous monitoring against defined thresholds.
- Leaving fraud accounts in the development sample. A fraudulent account is not a credit outcome, and including it teaches the scorecard to predict something it cannot observe, which is the data-quality link back to 4.2.
