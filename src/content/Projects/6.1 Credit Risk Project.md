## Project 1: Credit card application scorecard, start to end

An application scorecard ranks new applicants by their probability of going "bad" within 12 months of booking, and converts that into points used to approve, decline and assign limits. The lifecycle runs in 11 stages; for each, know what you did and why.

### 1. Business problem and objective

- **Objective:** predict P(bad within 12 months on book) at the moment of application, to drive approve/decline, cutoff and credit-limit decisions.
- **Stakeholders:** credit policy owns the cutoffs, risk analytics builds the model, model validation reviews it independently, engineering deploys it in the decision engine.
- **Why a WoE logistic scorecard rather than only XGBoost:** every decline needs explainable reasons, model-risk teams expect transparency, a points table is trivial to deploy in a rules engine, and it stays stable over time.
- **Success criteria fixed up front:** for example, holdout Gini of at least 0.45, monotonic rank-ordering across deciles, and PSI under 0.1.

### 2. Data sources

| Source | Example fields | Home Credit table |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application form | Age, income, employment tenure, residence type, education | application\_train |
| Credit bureau | Active loans, enquiries, max DPD, write-offs, oldest tradeline | bureau, bureau\_balance |
| Internal history | Past applications, past card and instalment behaviour | previous\_application, credit\_card\_balance, installments\_payments |
| External scores | Bureau-style risk scores | EXT\_SOURCE\_1/2/3 columns |

In India the bureau pull comes from CIBIL, Experian, Equifax or CRIF High Mark. The dataset has 307,511 applications with an 8.1% bad rate.

### 3. Target definition (the most-asked topic)

- **Observation point:** the application date. **Observation window:** the bureau lookback used for features, typically 12 to 24 months before application.
- **Performance window:** 12 months after booking for cards. Chosen by **vintage analysis**: plot cumulative bad rate by months on book (MOB) for several monthly cohorts and pick the MOB where curves flatten. Cards usually stabilise around 12 to 18 MOB.
- **Bad definition:** chosen by **roll-rate analysis**. Build a matrix of worst DPD in the 6 months before a reference date against worst DPD in the 6 months after. Pick the delinquency level beyond which most accounts roll forward instead of curing.
- **Typical final definition:** Bad = ever 90+ DPD within 12 MOB, or charged off, written off or settled. Good = never reached 30 DPD. Indeterminate = worst DPD of 30 to 89, excluded from development.
- **Exclusions:** confirmed fraud (owned by fraud models, which is your strength), deceased, staff and VIP accounts, policy declines, and cards never activated (insufficient performance).

Illustrative roll-rate read (your numbers will differ):

| Worst DPD, past 6 months | Roll forward to worse | Cure to current |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1-29 | 15% | 60% |
| 30-59 | 35% | 40% |
| 60-89 | 65% | 20% |
| 90+ | 85% | 5% |

Once an account reaches 60 to 89 DPD, most roll forward, so 90+ is the "point of no return" and a defensible bad definition.

**What Home Credit gives you:** a ready-made TARGET (payment difficulty on early instalments). Run your own roll-rate and vintage analysis on credit\_card\_balance, which has monthly DPD per card, so you can explain how you would set the definition at a lender.

### 4. Sampling, splits and reject inference

- **Development sample:** booked accounts from a 6 to 12 month application window, covering seasonality, all with matured performance.
- **Validation:** a 30% random holdout plus an **out-of-time (OOT)** sample from a later cohort. OOT is the real test of stability.
- Home Credit has no application dates, so use a stratified 70/30 split with 5-fold cross-validation, and say plainly that you would add OOT at a lender.
- **Imbalance:** an 8% bad rate is fine for logistic regression. Avoid SMOTE; it distorts WoE and calibration. If you down-sample goods, correct the intercept by adding ln(goods sampling fraction).
- **Reject inference:** you only observe performance for approved applicants, so the model never learns how rejects would behave. Methods include hard cutoff augmentation, parceling, fuzzy augmentation (each reject added twice, weighted by P(good) and P(bad)), and in India the strongest option: checking rejected applicants' bureau performance on loans they took elsewhere.
- Home Credit has no outcomes for rejects, so state this as a known limitation rather than claiming you did it.

### 5. Feature engineering

- **Bureau depth:** number of active tradelines, unsecured loan count, age of oldest tradeline, thin-file flag.
- **Delinquency:** max DPD in last 6/12/24 months, count of 30+ episodes in 12 months, months since last delinquency, any write-off or settlement.
- **Credit hunger:** enquiries in last 1/3/6 months, new loans opened in last 6 months. This is velocity logic you already know from fraud.
- **Utilisation:** total card balance divided by total card limit, number of cards above 80% utilisation.
- **Capacity:** FOIR (existing EMIs divided by monthly income), credit-to-income, and annuity-to-income in Home Credit.
- **Stability:** age, employment tenure, years at current address. Never use protected attributes such as gender or religion, even if predictive.
- **Your edge:** an H3-grid default rate as a geo-risk feature, linking directly to your OneCard work.
- **Missing values** stay as their own bin. A missing bureau record often means new-to-credit, which is informative.

### 6. Binning, WoE and IV

- **Fine classing:** about 20 quantile bins per variable. **Coarse classing:** merge into 3 to 7 bins, each holding at least 5% of the population, with a monotonic or business-logical bad-rate trend.
- Use the optbinning library (OptimalBinning with a monotonic trend constraint) to do this systematically.

```latex
\mathrm{WoE}_i = \ln\left(\frac{\%\,\mathrm{Good}_i}{\%\,\mathrm{Bad}_i}\right)
```

```latex
\mathrm{IV} = \sum_i \left(\%\,\mathrm{Good}_i - \%\,\mathrm{Bad}_i\right)\times \mathrm{WoE}_i
```

| IV | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| Below 0.02 | Not predictive, drop |
| 0.02 to 0.1 | Weak |
| 0.1 to 0.3 | Medium |
| 0.3 to 0.5 | Strong |
| Above 0.5 | Suspicious, check for leakage |

**Why WoE:** it handles missing values and outliers, makes each variable linear in log-odds, puts all variables on one scale, and enforces business-sensible patterns.

### 7. Variable selection and model fit

- **Filters:** IV above 0.02; among WoE variables with correlation above 0.6, keep the higher-IV one; VIF under 3; stable variable PSI.
- **Model:** logistic regression on WoE values (statsmodels for p-values), with stepwise or L1 selection to shortlist.
- **Checks:** all p-values under 0.05, and all coefficients share one sign (negative when WoE = ln(good/bad) and target bad = 1). A flipped sign signals multicollinearity, so drop that variable.
- **Shape of the final model:** 8 to 15 variables drawn from different information types, not five delinquency variables, and no single variable dominating the score range.
- **Challenger:** LightGBM on raw features with SHAP. It usually wins a few Gini points; use it as a benchmark and to spot missed interactions.

### 8. Scaling to points

```latex
\text{Factor} = \frac{\text{PDO}}{\ln 2}, \qquad \text{Offset} = \text{Score}_{\text{target}} - \text{Factor}\times\ln(\text{Odds}_{\text{target}})
```

```latex
\text{Points}_{ij} = -\left(\beta_i\,\mathrm{WoE}_{ij} + \frac{\beta_0}{n}\right)\times\text{Factor} + \frac{\text{Offset}}{n}
```

- Here i is the variable, j its bin, n the number of variables, and beta\_0 the intercept.
- **Worked example:** 600 points at 50:1 good:bad odds with PDO 20 gives Factor = 20 / 0.693 = 28.85 and Offset = 600 - 28.85 x 3.912 = 487.1.
- **One attribute:** with beta = -0.8, WoE = 0.5, intercept = -2.44 (about ln(8/92)) and n = 10, points = -(-0.4 - 0.244) x 28.85 + 48.7, which is about 67.
- **Interpretation:** every 20 points doubles the odds, so 620 means 100:1 and 580 means 25:1. The deliverable is a table of variable, bin and integer points.

### 9. Validation

| Metric | What it tells you | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gini (2 x AUC - 1) | Discrimination | 0.40 to 0.60 for application scorecards |
| KS | Max gap between cumulative good and bad distributions | 0.30 to 0.50 |
| Rank ordering | Bad rate by score decile | Must fall monotonically, no breaks |
| Capture rate | Share of bads in the worst 10% and 30% of scores | Higher is better; report it |
| Calibration | Predicted vs actual bad rate by decile | Close match; Hosmer-Lemeshow test |
| PSI | Score distribution shift, development vs recent | Under 0.1 stable, 0.1 to 0.25 watch, above 0.25 investigate |
| CSI | The same shift, per variable | Explains what drove PSI |
| Holdout and OOT drop | Overfitting | Relative Gini drop under about 10% |

```latex
\mathrm{PSI} = \sum_i (A_i - E_i)\times\ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right)
```

A\_i is the share of recent applicants in score band i and E\_i the share in development. These thresholds are industry rules of thumb and vary by lender.

### 10. Strategy: cutoffs and credit limits

- **Cutoff:** build a strategy table of approval rate and expected bad rate at each candidate cutoff. Set it where the marginal band's expected loss exceeds its expected revenue, or to hit a target bad rate or approval rate.
- **Expected loss** per account = PD x LGD x EAD. For cards, EAD = drawn balance + CCF x undrawn limit, since customers draw down lines before defaulting.
- **Swap-set analysis** against the old model at the same approval rate: swap-outs (old approves, new declines) should have a clearly higher bad rate than swap-ins (old declines, new approves).
- **Policy knock-outs** run before the score: age outside policy range, bureau score below a floor, current DPD, write-off in last 24 months, enquiry spikes, negative pincodes or H3 grids, and device or identity fraud flags.
- **Credit limit matrix:** score band x income band sets the limit, for example as a multiple of monthly income that rises with better bands and higher income.
- **Champion-challenger:** route 10 to 20% of traffic to the new model or cutoff before full rollout.

### 11. Deployment and monitoring

- **Deployment:** load the points table into the decision engine. Feature definitions must match training exactly (bureau pull timing, missing handling); mismatch is the top cause of live failures.
- **Shadow run:** score live applicants for 2 to 4 weeks without acting on the score, and compare the distribution with development using PSI.
- **Reason codes:** the variables where an applicant lost the most points against the maximum become decline reasons.
- **Front-end monitoring** (monthly from day one): PSI, CSI, approval rate, and override rates (low-side = approved below cutoff, high-side = declined above it).
- **Back-end monitoring** (as accounts season): early reads like 3 MOB 30+ and 6 MOB 60+ by score band, then full Gini and KS at 12 MOB, plus actual vs expected bad rate per band.
- **Triggers:** PSI above 0.25 or a relative Gini drop above about 10% leads to recalibration (refit intercept and scaling) or a rebuild. Expect an annual review and independent validation documentation.

## Project 1: Interview questions and answers

Open with a 60-second pitch, then expect the interviewer to drill into target definition, WoE, validation and strategy.

**60-second pitch:** "I built a credit card application scorecard on the Home Credit dataset of 307K applications. I validated the bad definition with roll-rate and vintage analysis, engineered \[X\] bureau and application features, and used monotonic WoE binning with IV and VIF filters. The final logistic scorecard has \[X\] variables, scaled to 600 points at 50:1 odds with PDO 20, with holdout Gini of \[X\]. I then built a cutoff strategy, a limit-assignment matrix and a monitoring plan."

**Q: How did you define bad, and why a 12-month window?** The dataset has a predefined target, but I validated the logic myself. Roll-rate analysis on monthly card DPD showed most 60-89 DPD accounts roll forward, so 90+ is the point of no return. Vintage curves flatten around 12 MOB, so a shorter window would miss bads and a longer one would delay the model.

**Q: Why logistic regression instead of XGBoost?** Declines need clear reasons, model-risk teams need transparency, and a points table deploys easily in a rules engine. My LightGBM challenger gained \[X\] Gini points, which I judged not worth the loss in explainability and stability. A hybrid is possible: GBM insights feed new engineered features into the scorecard.

**Q: A variable shows IV of 0.7. What do you do?** Treat it as suspicious and check for leakage first: is it captured after the application date, or derived from the outcome? If it is genuine, like a bureau score, I keep it but check it isn't dominating the score, or use it for segmentation.

**Q: A variable's WoE isn't monotonic. What then?** Merge adjacent bins until the trend is monotonic. I allow a non-monotonic shape only when it has business logic, such as zero utilisation (an inactive card) being riskier than moderate utilisation.

**Q: A coefficient has the wrong sign. Why?** Almost always multicollinearity: the variable's effect is being absorbed by a correlated one. I check correlation and VIF, then drop the weaker variable.

**Q: Gini or KS, which matters more?** Gini measures ranking across the whole distribution; KS measures the maximum separation at a single score point. I report both, but Gini is the standard headline metric, while KS is useful near the cutoff.

**Q: PSI jumps to 0.3 three months after go-live. What do you do?** First rule out data issues such as a bureau format change or a broken feature pipeline, then use CSI to find which variables shifted. Next check business causes like a new sourcing channel, and whether early delinquency reads show real degradation. Recalibrate if ranking holds but levels moved; rebuild if ranking broke.

**Q: Did you do reject inference?** No, because the dataset has no outcomes for rejected applicants. At a lender, I would first use bureau performance of rejects on loans taken elsewhere, then fall back to fuzzy augmentation, and test whether it changes the variable selection.

**Q: Application vs behavioural scorecard?** An application scorecard scores at origination using bureau and application data, with no on-us history. A behavioural scorecard scores existing customers monthly using payment, utilisation and spend patterns, drives limit increases and collections, and usually has a higher Gini.

**Q: How do you handle new-to-credit applicants?** Build a separate thin-file segment model using alternate data: bank statements via the Account Aggregator framework, employment and salary data, and device signals. Then start them on lower limits with a test-and-learn approach.

**Q: How do you set the cutoff?** From a strategy table of approval rate against expected bad rate, combined with expected profit per band and the lender's risk appetite. I confirm the choice with swap-set analysis against the current model.

**Q: How do fraud and credit risk interact here?** First-party fraud, such as never-pay or bust-out, looks like credit default in the data. I'd exclude confirmed fraud from the bad definition and watch first-payment default separately, which my fraud work at OneCard prepared me for.

**Q: Why does calibration matter if ranking is good?** Cutoffs only need ranking, but pricing, expected credit loss and limit sizing use the absolute PD. A model that ranks well but predicts 5% when reality is 8% will under-price and under-provision.

## Project 2: Personal loan risk-based pricing, start to end

Risk-based pricing sets each borrower's rate so the loan covers funding cost, operating cost, expected loss and capital, still earns a target return, and stays competitive. The lifecycle runs in 10 stages.

### 1. Business problem and objective

- **Objective:** a rate card (risk band x term x amount) where every cell meets a hurdle return, replacing flat or judgment-based pricing.
- **Why it matters:** a flat rate overcharges good borrowers, who leave for competitors, and undercharges risky ones, who flock to you. This adverse selection is what risk-based pricing fixes.
- **Stakeholders:** credit risk (PD and LGD), treasury and finance (cost of funds, capital), business (volumes, competitor rates), pricing committee or ALCO (approval), compliance (disclosure).
- **India context:** lenders need a board-approved interest rate policy and must disclose the all-in APR in a Key Fact Statement. Verify the latest RBI circulars before interviews.

### 2. Pricing framework

The simple annualised build-up, useful for explaining the idea:

```latex
r = (1-K)\times \mathrm{CoF} + K\times \mathrm{Hurdle} + \mathrm{Opex} + \mathrm{PD}\times\mathrm{LGD} - \mathrm{Fee\ yield}
```

K is the capital held as a share of the loan; the rest is debt-funded. Illustrative numbers for a mid-risk band, 36 months, Rs 3 lakh:

| Component | Assumption | % per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Funding cost | 82% debt-funded x 9.0% cost of funds | 7.38 |
| Cost of capital | 18% capital x 20% pre-tax hurdle | 3.60 |
| Operating cost | Origination and servicing | 2.50 |
| Expected loss | 4.0% annual PD x 80% LGD | 3.20 |
| Processing fee | 2% upfront spread over about 1.5 balance-years | -1.30 |
| **Required rate** |  | **15.38** |

The 18% capital roughly reflects a 125% risk weight on unsecured consumer loans times 15% capital adequacy; confirm current norms. The rigorous version, which is what you build, replaces this table with a monthly cash-flow NPV (stage 7).

### 3. Data and definitions (Lending Club)

- About 2.26M loans issued 2007 to 2018, with fields like issue\_d, term, funded\_amnt, int\_rate, grade, loan\_status, fico\_range\_low, dti, annual\_inc, revol\_util, inq\_last\_6mths and purpose.
- **Use matured loans only:** 36-month loans issued by end-2015 have completed their term. Loans still "Current" are right-censored; counting them as good understates PD. Survival analysis is the alternative that keeps them.
- **Default** = Charged Off or Default status; **good** = Fully Paid. Drop the "Does not meet the credit policy" rows.
- **Leakage (a classic interview trap):** drop every post-origination field from the PD model, such as total\_pymnt, recoveries, last\_pymnt\_d, out\_prncp and last\_fico\_range.
- Also exclude int\_rate, grade and sub\_grade. They are Lending Club's own pricing output, so using them would just copy its pricing.

### 4. PD model and risk bands

- **Model:** logistic regression or LightGBM predicting default over the 36-month term, using FICO, DTI, income, utilisation, enquiries, delinquencies, employment length, loan-to-income and purpose.
- **Risk bands:** cut scores into about 7 bands with monotonically rising default rates and enough loans in each.
- **Calibration matters more than in the scorecard:** price depends on the PD level, not just ranking. Compare predicted vs actual default per band and recalibrate (Platt, isotonic, or band-level actuals).
- **Through-the-cycle adjustment:** 2012 to 2015 was a benign credit period, so add a downturn or forward-looking overlay so prices hold in a worse economy.
- **Validation:** Gini, KS, band-level calibration with a binomial test. Expect a lower Gini than the bureau-rich scorecard; report your own figure.

### 5. Default timing and prepayment curves

- **Why timing matters:** an early default loses more principal, and a prepayment cuts interest income. Whether and when both matter for price.
- For each loan, derive the month it stopped paying (from last\_pymnt\_d minus issue\_d) and the month of any early payoff.
- **Monthly default hazard** per band = loans defaulting in month t divided by loans alive at the start of month t. Prepayment hazard is the same and is called SMM (single monthly mortality); CPR = 1 - (1 - SMM)^12.
- **Tools:** lifelines (Kaplan-Meier, or Aalen-Johansen for competing risks), or a discrete-time hazard model (logistic regression on loan-month rows).
- **Insight to mention:** low-risk borrowers prepay more because they can refinance elsewhere, which shortens their loans and erodes your yield on the best customers.

### 6. LGD and EAD

- **EAD** for an amortising loan = outstanding principal at default, from the amortisation schedule. In Lending Club, EAD is about funded\_amnt minus total\_rec\_prncp.
- **LGD** is computed per defaulted loan, ideally with recoveries discounted for the months they take to arrive:

```latex
\mathrm{LGD} = 1 - \frac{\text{recoveries} - \text{collection fee}}{\mathrm{EAD}}
```

- Unsecured LGD is high, and Lending Club recoveries are small, so expect a pooled LGD well above 80%. Segment LGD only if bands differ materially.

### 7. Cash-flow NPV and breakeven rate

For each band and term, project a pool of loans month by month at a candidate rate r. S is the share still alive, B the scheduled balance, h\_d and h\_p the default and prepayment hazards:

```latex
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{CF}_t] = S_{t-1}\Big[(1-h_d(t)-h_p(t))\,\mathrm{EMI} + h_p(t)\,B_{t-1}\big(1+\tfrac{r}{12}\big) + h_d(t)\,(1-\mathrm{LGD})\,B_{t-1}\Big]
```

```latex
\mathrm{NPV}(r) = -L + F - C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{CF}_t] - S_{t-1}\,\mathrm{Opex}_t}{(1+d)^t}, \qquad S_t = S_{t-1}\,(1-h_d(t)-h_p(t))
```

- L is the loan amount, F the processing fee, C\_0 the origination cost, and d the monthly hurdle discount rate blending funding and capital cost.
- Solve NPV(r\*) = 0 with scipy.optimize.brentq. r\* is the minimum rate that meets the hurdle for that band and term.
- The output is a breakeven rate grid of band x term, the floor of your rate card.

### 8. From breakeven to market rate

- **Breakeven is a floor, not the price.** The final rate also reflects competitor rates and borrower price sensitivity.
- **Take-up model:** P(accept | rate, band, gap to competitor) is estimated from offer-level data, ideally from randomised price tests. Lending Club has no declined offers, so simulate with an assumed elasticity and say so openly.
- **Optimise per band:** maximise expected profit = P(accept | r) x NPV(r).
- **Constraints:** r at or above breakeven; a riskier band never cheaper than a safer one; a policy rate cap; a 0.25% rate grid; and the EMI at r keeping FOIR within the affordability cap.
- **Adverse selection by price:** higher rates drive away good borrowers first, so the accepting pool gets riskier. Monitor the PD of acceptors at higher price points.

### 9. Back-testing and stress testing

- **Realised return by sub-grade:** approximate each Lending Club sub-grade's loss-adjusted annual return from total payments, funded amount and loan life, then compare with your breakeven to see where it under- or over-priced.
- **Rate comparison:** set your band rate against Lending Club's actual int\_rate for the same borrowers.
- **Sensitivity:** how much the rate moves for PD +/-20%, LGD +/-10 points, cost of funds +100 bps and prepayment speed +/-25%. A tornado chart of these is a strong README visual.
- **Stress:** apply PD x 1.5 as a recession scenario and check which bands still clear the hurdle.

### 10. Deployment and monitoring

- **Rate card** in the loan origination system as a lookup on band x term x amount, approved by the pricing committee, with capped discretionary discounts under an approval matrix.
- **Price testing:** champion-challenger of +/-25 to 50 bps within a band to learn real elasticity.
- **Monitoring:** actual vs expected early delinquency per band (3 MOB 30+, 6 MOB 60+, vintage curves), prepayment speed vs assumption, take-up by band, mix shift toward riskier bands, portfolio yield, NIM and risk-adjusted margin.
- **Repricing triggers:** a repo rate or funding cost change, PD drift beyond tolerance, competitor moves, or a regulatory capital change.

## Project 2: Interview questions and answers

Pricing interviews test whether you understand where each percentage point of the rate comes from, and why timing matters.

**60-second pitch:** "I built a risk-based pricing engine for unsecured personal loans on Lending Club data. On \[X\]K matured 36-month loans, I built a leakage-free PD model with \[7\] calibrated bands, estimated monthly default and prepayment curves and an LGD of \[X\]%, and combined them in a cash-flow NPV model to solve for the hurdle-meeting rate per band and term. Back-testing against Lending Club's actual rates showed \[finding\], and I stress-tested the rate card under recession scenarios."

**Q: What goes into a loan's interest rate?** Cost of funds, operating cost, expected loss (PD x LGD), and the cost of capital held against unexpected loss, minus fee income. Then a market layer adjusts the rate for competition and price sensitivity.

**Q: Why not just add PD x LGD to cost of funds?** That ignores timing. The balance amortises, defaults happen at different months, prepayments cut interest income, and upfront fees are earned over a shorter life when loans prepay. A monthly cash-flow NPV captures all of these.

**Q: Why did you exclude int\_rate and grade from the PD model?** They are Lending Club's own pricing output. Using them would make my PD model copy their pricing, and the whole point was to price independently and then compare.

**Q: How did you treat loans still running?** I used only matured cohorts, 36-month loans issued by end-2015. Counting active loans as good would understate PD because they haven't had time to default. Survival analysis is the alternative that keeps them as censored observations.

**Q: How does prepayment affect pricing?** It cuts interest income and shortens the life over which you recover acquisition cost. Low-risk borrowers prepay most because they can refinance, so ignoring prepayment over-states the profitability of your best band.

**Q: Expected loss vs unexpected loss?** Expected loss is the average loss, a cost of doing business priced directly into the rate. Unexpected loss is the volatility around it, covered by capital, and its cost enters the rate through the hurdle return on that capital.

**Q: What is RAROC?** Risk-adjusted return on capital: (revenue - funding cost - opex - expected loss) divided by economic capital. A loan is priced acceptably if RAROC meets the hurdle rate, often around 18 to 20% pre-tax.

**Q: A competitor prices 2% below you in your best band. What do you do?** First check whether that rate is still above my breakeven. If it is, test a lower rate with a price experiment and weigh lifetime value such as cross-sell. If it isn't, I hold price: matching would grow volume at a loss.

**Q: Your PD model ranks well but is miscalibrated. Does it matter?** For pricing, yes. If predicted PD is 3% and actual is 5%, every loan in that band is under-priced. I check calibration per band and recalibrate before building the rate card.

**Q: Can a higher rate itself increase default?** Yes, in two ways. Good borrowers walk away first, so the accepting pool gets riskier (adverse selection). And a higher EMI raises the payment burden, so the same borrower becomes riskier. That's why I added a FOIR affordability constraint.

**Q: Point-in-time or through-the-cycle PD for pricing?** Through-the-cycle or forward-looking, so prices don't swing with every credit cycle. Point-in-time PD is used for expected credit loss provisioning under IFRS 9 or Ind AS 109.

**Q: How would you price without elasticity data?** Start from breakeven plus market positioning against competitor rates, then run randomised price tests of 25 to 50 bps within each band to estimate take-up response.

**Q: How does pricing link to the approval cutoff?** They are set together. A band that loses money at a standard rate might be profitable at a higher risk-based rate, so pricing can extend approvals, while bands that can't clear the hurdle even at the rate cap get declined.
