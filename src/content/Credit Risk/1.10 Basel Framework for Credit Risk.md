# 1.10 Basel Framework for Credit Risk

## Definition

The Basel framework sets the minimum regulatory capital a lender must hold against credit exposures. It offers two routes. Under the **Standardized Approach (SA)**, the supervisor prescribes a risk weight for each exposure class and the lender applies it - no internal models. Under the **Internal Ratings Based (IRB) approach**, the lender estimates PD, LGD, and EAD itself and feeds them into a supervisor-specified formula that outputs the risk weight. Retail exposures sit in defined sub-classes, and - unlike corporate exposures - retail has no Foundation IRB option: a bank adopting IRB for retail must estimate all three parameters.

## Why it matters

Risk weights determine how much equity a rupee of lending consumes, and therefore what a product must earn to be viable. The capital charge in the pricing build-up from 1.5 comes directly from here. Risk weights are also a policy instrument: RBI raised the weight on unsecured consumer credit from 100% to 125% in November 2023 specifically to slow unsecured growth, and the effect ran straight through to loan pricing across the industry. For a lender, the gap between SA and IRB outcomes on a well-underwritten retail book is large enough - often 20 to 40 percentage points of risk weight - to be a strategic question rather than a compliance one.

## Formula

Standardized Approach. Exposures are slotted into classes, each with a prescribed weight. For Indian retail lending the material ones are:

| Exposure class | Risk weight |
|---|---|
| Regulatory retail portfolio (qualifying claims) | 75% |
| Consumer credit - personal loans, consumer durables (banks and NBFCs) | 125% |
| Credit card receivables - banks | 150% |
| Credit card receivables - NBFCs | 125% |
| Individual housing loans | 35-75%, banded by LTV and ticket size |
| Loans secured by gold and gold jewellery | Concessional treatment |
| NPAs, net of specific provisions below 20% | 150% |

Consumer credit is explicitly carved out of the regulatory retail portfolio in India, so it does not get the 75% weight. Housing, education, vehicle loans, and gold-backed loans are excluded from the 125% consumer credit weight. Microfinance loans in the nature of consumer credit were moved back to 100% in February 2025. These bands are revised by circular and should be checked against the current RBI Basel III master direction rather than memorised.

To qualify for the regulatory retail portfolio at 75%, an exposure must satisfy four criteria: orientation (exposure to an individual or small business), product (revolving credits, personal term loans, leases, small business facilities - not securities), granularity (aggregate exposure to any one counterparty below 0.2% of the total regulatory retail portfolio), and low value (aggregate exposure to one counterparty within the prescribed threshold, currently ₹7.5 crore).

IRB Approach. The retail risk-weight function, which contains no maturity adjustment:

```
K = LGD x N[ (1-R)^-0.5 x G(PD) + (R / (1-R))^0.5 x G(0.999) ] - (PD x LGD)

RW  = K x 12.5
RWA = RW x EAD
```

where:
- `K` = capital requirement per unit of exposure, covering unexpected loss only
- `LGD` = downturn loss given default, in %
- `PD` = through-the-cycle probability of default, in %, subject to a 0.03% floor
- `R` = asset correlation, prescribed by retail sub-class
- `N[.]` = standard normal cumulative distribution function
- `G[.]` = inverse standard normal cumulative distribution function
- `G(0.999)` = 3.0902, the 99.9th percentile confidence level
- `12.5` = the reciprocal of the 8% minimum capital ratio, converting a capital requirement into a risk weight

The subtraction of `PD x LGD` is the key structural feature: expected loss is removed from the capital requirement because it is meant to be covered by provisions and pricing, not equity.

Correlations by retail sub-class:

```
Residential mortgage:              R = 0.15
Qualifying revolving retail (QRRE): R = 0.04
Other retail:  R = 0.03 x w + 0.16 x (1 - w)
               where w = (1 - e^(-35 x PD)) / (1 - e^(-35))
```

`w` runs from 1 at very low PD to near 0 at high PD, so correlation falls from 16% to 3% as PD rises. The logic is that high-PD retail borrowers default for idiosyncratic reasons, while low-PD borrowers default mainly when systematic conditions turn.

## Worked Example

Example: the personal loan portfolio from 1.5 - PD 4.10%, downturn LGD 72%, classified as other retail.

Correlation:

```
w = (1 - e^(-35 x 0.0410)) / (1 - e^(-35)) = (1 - e^(-1.435)) / 1 = 1 - 0.23814 = 0.76186
R = 0.03 x 0.76186 + 0.16 x 0.23814 = 0.02286 + 0.03810 = 0.06096
```

Conditional default rate at the 99.9th percentile:

```
G(0.0410)  = -1.7392
(1-R)^-0.5 = (0.93904)^-0.5 = 1.03217
(R/(1-R))^0.5 = (0.06492)^0.5 = 0.25479

Argument = 1.03217 x (-1.7392) + 0.25479 x 3.0902
         = -1.79514 + 0.78738 = -1.00776

N(-1.00776) = 0.15678, i.e. a 15.68% stressed default rate
```

Capital requirement and risk weight:

```
K  = 0.72 x 0.15678 - (0.0410 x 0.72)
   = 0.11288 - 0.02952 = 0.08336
RW = 0.08336 x 12.5 = 104.2%
```

Comparison at an EAD of ₹1,840 crore and a 15.5% total capital ratio:

| Approach | Risk weight | RWA | Capital held |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standardized (consumer credit) | 125.0% | ₹2,300 cr | ₹356.5 cr |
| IRB (other retail) | 104.2% | ₹1,917 cr | ₹297.1 cr |

IRB releases ₹59.4 crore of capital on this book - roughly 20 bps of pricing at a 16% required return on equity. Note also that the IRB expected loss of PD x LGD = 2.95% is deducted from capital against eligible provisions, which is the mechanical link back to 1.5.

Sensitivity to PD. Doubling PD from 4.10% to 8.20% raises `w` to 0.9433 and lowers R to 3.74%. The risk weight rises to roughly 121% - a 16 percentage point increase for a doubling of PD. The correlation adjustment deliberately dampens the response, which is why IRB retail risk weights are far less PD-sensitive than intuition suggests.

## How it connects

The three IRB inputs are exactly the parameters built in 1.2, 1.3, and 1.4, but calibrated differently: TTC PD rather than PIT, and downturn LGD rather than workout LGD. The expected loss subtraction ties directly to 1.5. Capital output feeds the pricing build-up in 1.7 and the stress-testing capital projections in 1.14. The SA treatment of NPAs links to RBI IRAC classification in 1.15. RBI has announced revised Basel III capital adequacy norms, including a new draft Standardized Approach for credit risk, to take effect from 1 April 2027 alongside the ECL framework.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Assuming IRB always reduces capital. It does for well-underwritten, low-PD books. On a high-PD unsecured portfolio with a genuine downturn LGD above 80%, IRB risk weights can exceed the standardized 125%. Very few Indian banks have received supervisory approval for retail IRB, so the SA remains the operating reality for most.
- Applying the 75% regulatory retail weight to consumer credit in India. It is explicitly excluded. This is a jurisdictional overlay on the Basel text and a common error when working from the international standard alone.
- Using PIT PD in the IRB formula. The formula assumes a through-the-cycle input; feeding it a point-in-time PD makes regulatory capital procyclical, which is precisely what the framework is designed to avoid.
- Confusing the 99.9% confidence level with a plausible scenario. It is a one-in-a-thousand-year calibration used to size capital, not a stress scenario anyone forecasts.
- Ignoring the correlation function's shape. Because R falls as PD rises, the risk weight is a concave function of PD. Portfolio managers who assume linearity misprice the capital impact of moving down the credit spectrum.
