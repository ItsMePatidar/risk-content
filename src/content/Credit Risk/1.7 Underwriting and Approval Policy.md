# 1.7 Underwriting and Approval Policy

## Definition

Underwriting is the decision layer that converts an application into an outcome: decline, approve, or approve on modified terms. It has three components that operate in sequence - policy rules (hard eligibility conditions that produce an automatic decline), scorecard decisioning (a cutoff applied to the PD estimate), and affordability assessment (whether the borrower's income can carry the instalment alongside existing obligations). A **policy rule engine** is the software layer that executes these deterministically, so that two identical applications receive identical decisions regardless of who processed them.

## Why it matters

Underwriting is where the lender's risk appetite becomes an actual number. Everything upstream is intention; everything downstream is consequence management. A poorly specified policy produces two distinct failure modes: rules that duplicate what the scorecard already prices (declining good customers twice for the same reason) and gaps that let unpriced risk through. Consistency is also a supervisory matter. RBI's digital lending directions require that the credit decision rest with the regulated entity, not with a lending service provider or an outsourced platform, which makes an auditable rule engine a compliance artefact and not just an operational convenience. High or unmonitored deviation rates - approvals granted outside policy by manual override - are the standard early indicator that underwriting discipline has been subordinated to volume targets.

## Formula

The decision waterfall, in the order rules should fire:

1. Deduplication and KYC - existing relationship check, negative list, identity verification.
2. Policy knockouts - age, minimum income, employment stability, geography, existing write-off or settlement flag with any lender.
3. Bureau assessment - score, DPD history, enquiry velocity, active trade lines.
4. Scorecard decision - PD estimate against cutoff.
5. Affordability - FOIR or DTI test, and for secured products LTV.
6. Limit and price assignment.
7. Verification - income documents, field investigation, employer check, triggered selectively by risk tier.

The affordability test:

```
FOIR = (Existing EMIs + Proposed EMI) / Net Monthly Income

Eligible EMI = (NMI x FOIR_cap) - Existing EMIs

Eligible loan = EMI x [ ((1+r)^n - 1) / (r x (1+r)^n) ]
```

where:
- `FOIR` = fixed obligations to income ratio, in %
- `Existing EMIs` = all servicing obligations visible on the bureau report plus declared informal debt, in ₹ per month; for revolving lines the convention is to include the minimum amount due, typically 5% of outstanding
- `NMI` = net monthly income after statutory deductions, in ₹
- `FOIR_cap` = the policy ceiling, in %; typically 45-50% at lower incomes and 55-65% at higher incomes, since absolute residual income matters more than the ratio
- `r` = periodic interest rate, in % per month
- `n` = tenor, in months

For secured products, the parallel constraint is loan to value:

```
LTV = Sanctioned amount / Assessed collateral value
```

where `Assessed collateral value` is the lower of the valuation report and the transaction value, in ₹. Both the affordability and the LTV constraint must be satisfied; the binding one determines the sanction.

## Worked Example

Example: a personal loan application for ₹6,00,000 over 48 months at 16.5%.

Applicant: net monthly income ₹78,500, existing auto loan EMI ₹14,200, credit card outstanding ₹1,48,000 against a ₹2,40,000 limit. Policy FOIR cap for this income band is 55%, and card obligations are counted at 5% of outstanding.

```
Card obligation counted = ₹1,48,000 x 5% = ₹7,400
Existing EMIs = ₹14,200 + ₹7,400 = ₹21,600

Proposed EMI = 6,00,000 x 0.01375 x 1.92610 / 0.92610 = ₹17,157

FOIR = (₹21,600 + ₹17,157) / ₹78,500 = 49.37%
```

Inside the 55% cap, so affordability passes. Maximum sanction under the cap:

```
Eligible EMI  = (₹78,500 x 55%) - ₹21,600 = ₹43,175 - ₹21,600 = ₹21,575
Eligible loan = ₹21,575 x [ (1.92610 - 1) / (0.01375 x 1.92610) ]
              = ₹21,575 x 34.968 = ₹7,54,400
```

Affordability supports ₹7,54,400 against a request of ₹6,00,000, so it is not the binding constraint. The scorecard is. If this is the applicant from 1.2 scoring 587 against a 600 cutoff, the decision moves to price, not to decline.

Swap-set economics of a cutoff change. Consider lowering the cutoff from 600 to 590. Approval rate rises from 34.0% to 39.2%. The incremental accounts have a PD of 6.80% against the portfolio's 4.10%:

```
Marginal EL   = 6.80% x 72% = 4.90%
Marginal yield                = 17.60%
Less: cost of funds           = (7.90%)
Less: operating cost          = (2.38%)
Less: expected loss           = (4.90%)
Less: capital charge          = (1.57%)
Marginal RAROA                = 0.85%
```

Positive, but thin. At a 590 cutoff the incremental business earns 85 bps pre-tax against 437 bps on the core book. That is a legitimate business decision - but it should be made explicitly, on these numbers, and not arrive as a byproduct of a volume push. Repricing the 590-599 band at 19.5% instead of 16.5% lifts marginal RAROA to 3.85% and is usually the better answer than either accepting or declining at a single price.

## How it connects

Underwriting consumes the PD from 1.2 and the scorecard from 1.6, and its cutoff decision is evaluated using the EL and pricing build-up from 1.5. The FOIR calculation depends on bureau trade-line data (1.6), and its accuracy determines whether multi-lending is detected. Every underwriting decision creates a cohort whose performance is measured by vintage analysis (1.8), which is the only honest feedback on whether a policy change worked. ## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Computing FOIR on gross rather than net income. The two differ by 10-25% depending on statutory deductions, and mixing them across segments makes the cap meaningless.
- Using FOIR alone at low incomes. A 50% FOIR on ₹22,000 income leaves ₹11,000 for a household; the same ratio on ₹2,20,000 leaves ₹1,10,000. Residual income floors must sit alongside the ratio.
- Stacking policy rules on top of a scorecard that already prices the same risk. If the scorecard includes DPD history, an additional hard knockout on any past 30 DPD declines accounts the model has already accounted for, reducing approval rate without reducing loss rate proportionally.
- Leaving deviation authority unmeasured. Override volume, override performance, and the identity of the approving authority should be reported monthly. Accounts approved on deviation typically default at 1.8-2.5x the policy-approved rate; if they do not, the policy is too tight.
- Treating verification as a fixed cost. Field investigation and employer verification are risk-tiered controls. Applying them uniformly wastes money on low-risk applicants and provides no incremental protection where it is actually needed.
