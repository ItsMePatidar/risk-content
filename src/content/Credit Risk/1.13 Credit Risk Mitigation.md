# 1.13 Credit Risk Mitigation

## Definition

**Credit risk mitigation (CRM)** covers the techniques a lender uses to reduce loss when a borrower defaults, without changing the probability that default occurs. In retail lending the main forms are collateral (mortgage on property, hypothecation of a vehicle, pledge of gold, lien on a deposit), third-party support (co-applicant, guarantor, credit guarantee scheme), and insurance (credit life cover on the borrower, asset insurance on the financed vehicle or property). CRM acts on LGD and EAD, not on PD - a guarantor does not make the primary borrower more likely to pay, it gives the lender a second source of recovery.

## Why it matters

Mitigation is what makes low-rate retail lending possible. A home loan at 8.6% cannot support an 84% LGD; it works because enforceable security compresses LGD into the 15-25% range. The word doing the work there is enforceable. Mitigation that fails at the moment it is needed - a guarantor who cannot be traced, a vehicle that was sold before repossession, a title defect discovered at enforcement - provides zero protection while having justified a lower price for the entire life of the loan. That asymmetry is why regulators impose eligibility conditions on recognised collateral, and why the operational quality of security creation and perfection matters more than the valuation on file.

## Formula

For secured retail, the mitigation effect flows through LGD:

```
LGD = max[ 0, 1 - ( V x (1 - h) x (1 - c) x DF ) / EAD ]
```

where:
- `V` = realisable collateral value at enforcement, in ₹ - not the value at origination
- `h` = distress-sale haircut, in %, the discount to market value achieved in a forced sale
- `c` = enforcement and disposal costs as a share of proceeds, in %
- `DF` = discount factor over the enforcement timeline, reflecting how long the process takes
- `EAD` = exposure at default, in ₹

The `max[0, ...]` matters: where recovery exceeds the debt, LGD is zero and the surplus goes back to the borrower. The lender does not capture upside on collateral.

For financial collateral (a deposit lien, for example), Basel's comprehensive approach nets exposure against collateral with supervisory haircuts:

```
E* = max[ 0, E x (1 + He) - C x (1 - Hc - Hfx) ]
```

where `E` is exposure, `C` is collateral value, `He` is the exposure haircut, `Hc` is the collateral haircut, and `Hfx` is a currency-mismatch haircut - the last being irrelevant in domestic retail. For eligible guarantees, the substitution approach applies instead: the protected portion takes the guarantor's risk weight.

Regulatory LTV ceilings are the front-end expression of CRM in Indian retail:

| Product | LTV constraint |
|---|---|
| Individual housing loans | Banded by ticket size; risk weight rises with LTV |
| Gold loans up to ₹2.5 lakh | 85% |
| Gold loans ₹2.5-5 lakh | 80% |
| Gold loans above ₹5 lakh | 75% |
| Loan against property | Lender policy, typically 50-65% |

The gold loan tiering came in with RBI's 2025 gold loan directions, effective 1 April 2026, replacing a flat 75% cap. For bullet-repayment gold loans, LTV is now computed against the total amount repayable at maturity rather than the disbursed amount, which materially lowers the effective LTV at sanction.

## Worked Example

Example: a loan against property. Property valued at ₹92,00,000 at origination, sanctioned at 60% LTV, so ₹55,20,000 disbursed. The account defaults three years in with an outstanding of ₹49,80,000. EIR is 10.75% per annum (0.8958% per month). Enforcement under SARFAESI takes 26 months from default to sale.

Base case - property market flat, revalued at ₹86,00,000 after a modest 6.5% correction. Distress sale realises 78% of that value.

```
Sale proceeds     = ₹86,00,000 x 78%        = ₹67,08,000
Enforcement costs (notice, possession, auction, legal) = (₹3,10,000)
Net nominal                                  = ₹63,98,000
Discount factor   = 1 / (1.008958)^26        = 0.79304
PV of recovery                               = ₹50,73,900

LGD = max[0, 1 - 50,73,900 / 49,80,000] = 0%
```

Full recovery, with a ₹93,900 surplus returned to the borrower. This is the LTV cushion doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Downturn case - the same default occurring in a stressed property market, with values down 22% to ₹71,76,000 and the same distress discount.

```
Sale proceeds     = ₹71,76,000 x 78%        = ₹55,97,280
Enforcement costs                            = (₹3,10,000)
Net nominal                                  = ₹52,87,280
PV of recovery    = ₹52,87,280 x 0.79304    = ₹41,93,000

LGD = 1 - 41,93,000 / 49,80,000 = 15.80%
```

A 22% fall in collateral value moved LGD from 0% to 15.8%. This is the precise reason Basel requires downturn LGD as a separate estimate: collateral values fall in the same conditions that raise default rates, so base-case LGD systematically understates loss exactly when losses arrive.

Sensitivity to timeline. Holding the downturn property value constant but extending enforcement from 26 to 44 months:

```
Discount factor = 1 / (1.008958)^44 = 0.67465
PV of recovery  = ₹52,87,280 x 0.67465 = ₹35,67,000
LGD = 28.37%
```

Eighteen additional months of enforcement delay cost 1,257 bps of LGD without any further fall in property value. In secured retail, enforcement speed is worth as much as collateral cover.

## How it connects

CRM is the operational determinant of the LGD estimated in 1.3 and, through it, of expected loss (1.5) and loan pricing (1.7). LTV constraints are set at underwriting and are the binding sanction limit for secured products alongside FOIR. Under the Standardized Approach (1.10), mitigation appears as lower prescribed risk weights for housing loans banded by LTV; under IRB it appears through own LGD estimates. Enforcement is the deep-bucket end of the collections process in 1.12, and the asset classification consequences of a secured NPA are covered in 1.15.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Treating collateral as reducing PD. It does not. A borrower with a mortgaged property defaults at a rate driven by income, obligations, and behaviour. Collateral changes what happens after.
- Using origination valuation in LGD estimates. The relevant value is realisable value at enforcement, after market movement and distress discount. The gap between the two is the entire content of downturn LGD.
- Ignoring enforcement timelines. SARFAESI applies only to secured creditors and only above a minimum outstanding, and unsecured personal loans and credit cards have no equivalent - recovery there runs through Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, civil suits, Lok Adalat, or negotiated settlement, all slower and less certain.
- Counting a guarantor as full mitigation. A co-applicant whose income was used to compute eligibility is a repayment source; a guarantor with no independent means and no traced assets is a documentation formality. Recovery rates against retail guarantors are low enough that most lenders do not model them as mitigation at all.
- Overlooking that credit life insurance covers death and sometimes disability, not default. It removes a specific tail scenario, not general credit risk, and lapsed premiums silently remove even that.
