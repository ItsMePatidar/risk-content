# 1.4 Exposure at Default (EAD)

## Definition

**Exposure at default (EAD)** is the amount the lender expects to be owed at the moment an account defaults. For amortising term loans - personal, auto, home - it is largely mechanical: the scheduled outstanding balance at the default date plus interest accrued during the delinquency period. For revolving products - credit cards, overdrafts, undrawn LAP limits - it must be estimated, because borrowers heading into default reliably draw down available credit before they stop paying. The estimation device is the **credit conversion factor (CCF)**, the proportion of the currently undrawn limit expected to be drawn by the default date.

## Why it matters

On revolving portfolios, EAD is the parameter most often understated. A card book reporting exposure as current outstanding balances ignores the fact that a distressed cardholder will typically consume 55-70% of remaining headroom in the final 12 months. Provisions and capital computed on drawn balances alone will be short by exactly that amount. This is also a limit-management issue: every unused rupee of sanctioned limit is a contingent exposure the lender has already given away. Basel treats undrawn commitments as exposure for this reason, and IFRS9 requires ECL on the undrawn portion of revolving facilities.

## Formula

For revolving facilities:

```
EAD = Drawn + CCF x (Limit - Drawn)
```

where:
- `Drawn` = current outstanding balance, in ₹
- `Limit` = sanctioned credit limit, in ₹
- `Limit - Drawn` = undrawn headroom, in ₹
- `CCF` = credit conversion factor, in %, the expected share of headroom drawn between the observation date and default

CCF is estimated from historical defaulted accounts using a fixed-horizon cohort approach:

```
CCF = (B_default - B_obs) / (Limit_obs - B_obs)
```

where:
- `B_default` = balance at the default date, in ₹
- `B_obs` = balance at the observation date, typically 12 months before default, in ₹
- `Limit_obs` = sanctioned limit at the observation date, in ₹

For amortising term loans:

```
EAD = O_t x (1 + r)^m
O_t = P x (1+r)^t - EMI x [ ((1+r)^t - 1) / r ]
```

where:
- `O_t` = scheduled outstanding principal after t paid instalments, in ₹
- `P` = original principal, in ₹
- `r` = periodic contractual rate, in % per month
- `t` = number of instalments actually paid
- `m` = number of months of delinquency accrual before the default trigger, normally 3 for a 90+ DPD definition

## Worked Example

Example A - credit card, CCF estimation and application. A defaulted card account is observed 12 months before default:

```
Limit at observation  = ₹2,15,000
Balance at observation = ₹78,400
Balance at default     = ₹1,63,900

CCF = (1,63,900 - 78,400) / (2,15,000 - 78,400)
    = 85,500 / 1,36,600
    = 62.59%
```

Pooling several thousand such accounts by utilisation band gives a portfolio CCF. Suppose the 30-50% utilisation band yields 58.0%. Applying it to a live account with the same profile:

```
EAD = ₹78,400 + 0.58 x ₹1,36,600
    = ₹78,400 + ₹79,228
    = ₹1,57,628
```

Exposure is more than double the current balance. Computing ECL on ₹78,400 would understate the provision by 50%.

Example B - personal loan, amortising. The ₹4,80,000 / 16.5% / 36-month loan from 1.1, defaulting after 14 paid instalments (EMI ₹16,996, r = 1.375% per month):

```
O_14 = 4,80,000 x (1.01375)^14 - 16,996 x [ ((1.01375)^14 - 1) / 0.01375 ]
     = 4,80,000 x 1.210697 - 16,996 x 15.3234
     = ₹5,81,135 - ₹2,60,436
     = ₹3,20,699

EAD = ₹3,20,699 x (1.01375)^3 = ₹3,20,699 x 1.041818 = ₹3,34,110
```

The ₹13,411 difference is interest accrued across the three months of delinquency that precede the 90+ DPD trigger. It is real exposure and belongs in the ECL calculation, subject to the accounting treatment of interest on Stage 3 assets.

## How it connects

EAD is the third term in expected loss (1.5) and scales both provisions and capital. CCF estimation depends on the same default definition and observation window used for PD (1.2), and mis-alignment between the two produces silent double counting or gaps. Limit management on revolving books - the servicing lever from 1.1 - is the direct operational control on EAD, and behavioural scorecards (1.6) drive limit increase and decrease decisions. Under the Basel Standardized Approach (1.10), CCFs are prescribed by commitment type rather than estimated; Advanced IRB permits own estimates.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Reporting EAD as current balance on revolving products. This is the single most common source of provision shortfall in card portfolios.
- Allowing CCF above 100% or below 0%. Both occur in raw data - the first when limits are increased after the observation date, the second when the borrower pays down before defaulting. Both are usually artefacts of the limit-change treatment, and clipping them without diagnosis hides a data problem.
- Assuming limit cuts eliminate exposure. Cutting an unused limit on an account already showing stress often triggers the drawdown it was meant to prevent, and cutting after the borrower has drawn achieves nothing. CCF is highest precisely for the accounts where intervention comes too late.
- Ignoring accrued interest and fees on amortising loans. The three-month accrual to the 90+ DPD trigger adds 3-5% to EAD at typical unsecured rates.
- Pooling CCF across utilisation bands. Low-utilisation accounts have far more headroom to consume and behave differently from near-maxed accounts. A single portfolio CCF misallocates exposure between the two.
