# 1.6 Credit Bureau Data and Scoring Models

## Definition

A credit bureau report is a structured record of a borrower's obligations across lenders - accounts held, sanctioned amounts, current balances, payment history month by month, and the enquiries other lenders have made. In India the four bureaus are TransUnion CIBIL, Experian, Equifax, and CRIF High Mark. An **application scorecard** scores a new applicant using bureau data, application-form data, and channel information at the point of decision. A **behavioural scorecard** rescores an existing customer using the internal repayment and usage data accumulated since booking, and generally outperforms the application scorecard once six months of performance exists.

## Why it matters

Bureau data is the only view a lender has of obligations it did not originate. Without it, FOIR is computed on self-declared liabilities and multi-lending goes undetected - the failure pattern behind most unsecured retail stress episodes, where the same borrower services four personal loans and two BNPL lines from six lenders who each believe they hold the only exposure. Scorecard quality determines the entire risk-return frontier: a scorecard with a Gini of 0.28 forces the lender to decline good customers to hold loss rates down, while one at 0.48 allows the same loss rate at a materially higher approval rate. The regulator's interest is in data accuracy and dispute handling, since a mis-reported DPD flag can lock a borrower out of credit for years.

## Formula

Scorecard development bins each variable and replaces raw values with weight of evidence, which linearises the relationship with log-odds and makes it directly compatible with logistic regression.

```
WOE_i = ln( %Goods_i / %Bads_i )

IV = SUM over i of [ (%Goods_i - %Bads_i) x WOE_i ]
```

where:
- `WOE_i` = weight of evidence for bin i; positive means the bin is good-skewed
- `%Goods_i` = goods in bin i as a share of all goods in the sample, in %
- `%Bads_i` = bads in bin i as a share of all bads, in %
- `IV` = information value, the total predictive strength of the variable across all bins

Conventional IV interpretation: below 0.02 unpredictive, 0.02-0.10 weak, 0.10-0.30 medium, 0.30-0.50 strong, above 0.50 strong but requiring a leakage check.

Discrimination is measured with two statistics:

```
KS = max | Cum%Bads(s) - Cum%Goods(s) |
Gini = 2 x AUC - 1
```

where:
- `KS` = Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, the maximum separation between the cumulative good and bad distributions across scores, in %
- `s` = score cutoff at which the separation is evaluated
- `AUC` = area under the ROC curve
- `Gini` = Gini coefficient, ranging 0 (no discrimination) to 1 (perfect)

Typical retail benchmarks: application scorecards 0.35-0.55 Gini, behavioural scorecards 0.55-0.75, because internal payment behaviour is more informative than anything observable at application.

## Worked Example

Example: bureau score binning on a personal loan development sample of 50,000 booked accounts - 47,200 goods and 2,800 bads (5.60% bad rate), observed over a 12-month performance window with a 90+ DPD bad definition.

| Bureau score bin | Goods | Bads | Bad rate | %Goods | %Bads | WOE | IV contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Below 650 | 4,720 | 1,036 | 18.00% | 10.00% | 37.00% | -1.3083 | 0.3532 |
| 650-699 | 7,552 | 728 | 8.79% | 16.00% | 26.00% | -0.4855 | 0.0486 |
| 700-739 | 11,328 | 532 | 4.49% | 24.00% | 19.00% | +0.2336 | 0.0117 |
| 740-779 | 12,272 | 336 | 2.66% | 26.00% | 12.00% | +0.7732 | 0.1082 |
| 780 and above | 11,328 | 168 | 1.46% | 24.00% | 6.00% | +1.3863 | 0.2495 |
| Total | 47,200 | 2,800 | 5.60% | 100% | 100% | | **IV = 0.771** |

Worked line for the lowest bin:

```
%Goods = 4,720 / 47,200 = 10.00%
%Bads  = 1,036 / 2,800  = 37.00%
WOE    = ln(0.1000 / 0.3700) = ln(0.2703) = -1.3083
IV contribution = (0.1000 - 0.3700) x (-1.3083) = 0.3532
```

The bad rate falls monotonically across bins, which is the requirement for a usable binning. An IV of 0.771 is very high - for a bureau score used as a single characteristic this is expected, but it also warns that the variable will dominate the model and that any bureau data-quality issue transmits straight to the decision.

KS computation, accumulating from the worst bin:

| Bin | Cum %Bads | Cum %Goods | Separation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below 650 | 37.00% | 10.00% | 27.00% |
| 650-699 | 63.00% | 26.00% | **37.00%** |
| 700-739 | 82.00% | 50.00% | 32.00% |
| 740-779 | 94.00% | 76.00% | 18.00% |
| 780+ | 100.00% | 100.00% | 0.00% |

KS = 37.00%, occurring at the 700 boundary. Cutting at 700 declines 26.00% of goods to avoid 63.00% of bads - a 2.42:1 exchange rate, which is the number underwriting policy actually negotiates over.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Reading a very high IV as good news. IV above 0.5 on a non-bureau variable usually signals leakage - a field populated after the decision, or a proxy for the outcome itself. Check the data lineage before celebrating.
- Building on booked accounts only. The model will be scored on the full through-the-door population, which includes rejects. Without reject inference the scorecard's measured Gini is optimistic and its performance on marginal applicants is unknown.
- Ignoring enquiry velocity because it seems weak alone. Number of unsecured enquiries in the last 30 days has modest standalone IV but strong interaction with FOIR and thin-file status. It is one of the earliest observable signals of credit-seeking distress.
- Treating new-to-credit applicants as a scoring problem. They cannot be scored by a bureau-dominated model at all. They need a separate scorecard on alternate data, or a separate policy track with lower limits - not an imputed bureau score.
- Monitoring only outcome metrics. Gini degrades slowly and visibly; population stability shifts fast and silently. A PSI above 0.25 on the score distribution means the scored population has changed enough that the calibration is stale, regardless of what the Gini still says.
