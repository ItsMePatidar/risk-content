# 1.11 IFRS9 Expected Credit Loss

## Definition

IFRS9 - implemented in India as Ind AS 109 - replaces incurred-loss provisioning with a forward-looking **expected credit loss (ECL)** model. Every exposure sits in one of three stages. Stage 1 covers assets whose credit risk has not increased significantly since origination, and carries a provision equal to 12-month ECL. Stage 2 covers assets that have suffered a **significant increase in credit risk (SICR)**, and carries lifetime ECL. Stage 3 covers credit-impaired assets, also at lifetime ECL, with interest revenue computed on the net carrying amount rather than the gross. Movement from Stage 1 to Stage 2 is the single most consequential event in the model, because the provision jumps from a 12-month to a lifetime horizon in one step.

## Why it matters

Staging drives reported earnings. A cohort migrating from Stage 1 to Stage 2 can multiply its provision several times over with no default having occurred, which is why the SICR definition receives more audit and supervisory attention than any other element. The framework is also explicitly forward-looking: provisions must be probability-weighted across macroeconomic scenarios, so a deteriorating unemployment forecast increases provisions today. In India, NBFCs have reported under Ind AS 109 since 2018-19 while banks remained on RBI's incurred-loss IRAC norms. That divergence ends on 1 April 2027, when the RBI Expected Credit Loss Directions - finalised in April 2026 - take effect for scheduled commercial banks, with a glide path to 31 March 2031 to absorb the one-time provisioning impact.

## Formula

```
ECL = SUM over t of [ PD_marginal(t) x LGD(t) x EAD(t) x DF(t) ]

DF(t) = 1 / (1 + EIR)^t
```

where:
- `PD_marginal(t)` = unconditional probability of default in period t, that is, the conditional hazard multiplied by survival to the start of t, in %
- `LGD(t)` = point-in-time, forward-looking loss given default for period t, in %
- `EAD(t)` = expected exposure at the start of period t, following the amortisation schedule for term loans or the CCF-adjusted balance for revolvers, in ₹
- `DF(t)` = discount factor
- `EIR` = original effective interest rate on the instrument, in % per period - not the current market rate

Horizon by stage:

```
Stage 1: sum t = 1 to 12 months
Stage 2: sum t = 1 to end of expected life
Stage 3: sum t = 1 to end of expected life, PD = 100%
```

Staging triggers. SICR is assessed by comparing the lifetime PD at the reporting date against the lifetime PD expected at origination for the same remaining term. Days past due is a backstop, not the primary test: 30+ DPD is a rebuttable presumption of SICR (Stage 2), and 90+ DPD is a rebuttable presumption of default (Stage 3).

Scenario weighting. The ECL must be a probability-weighted average across at least three macroeconomic scenarios:

```
ECL_reported = SUM over s of [ P(s) x ECL(s) ]
```

where `P(s)` is the assigned probability of scenario s, summing to 100%. Because the PD-to-macro relationship is convex, the probability-weighted ECL exceeds the ECL computed on the base scenario alone - this difference is the forward-looking overlay, and it must not be netted away.

## Worked Example

Example: the personal loan from 1.4, now 14 months seasoned. Gross carrying amount ₹3,20,700, remaining term 22 months, EMI ₹16,996, EIR 16.5% per annum (1.375% per month), LGD 72%.

Marginal PD schedule, in six-month buckets:

| Bucket | Months | Conditional PD | Survival at start | Marginal (unconditional) PD |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1-6 | 2.30% | 100.00% | 2.3000% |
| 2 | 7-12 | 1.80% | 97.70% | 1.7586% |
| 3 | 13-18 | 1.45% | 95.94% | 1.3911% |
| 4 | 19-22 | 0.85% | 94.55% | 0.8037% |

Cumulative 12-month PD = 4.06%; lifetime PD = 6.25%.

Expected exposure at each bucket midpoint, from the amortisation schedule, with the discount factor at that point:

| Bucket | Midpoint (month) | EAD | Discount factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | ₹2,82,421 | 0.95985 |
| 2 | 9 | ₹2,00,987 | 0.88434 |
| 3 | 15 | ₹1,12,612 | 0.81478 |
| 4 | 20 | ₹33,160 | 0.76099 |

ECL contribution per bucket, computed as marginal PD x LGD x EAD x DF:

```
Bucket 1: 0.023000 x 0.72 x 2,82,421 x 0.95985 = ₹4,489
Bucket 2: 0.017586 x 0.72 x 2,00,987 x 0.88434 = ₹2,250
Bucket 3: 0.013911 x 0.72 x 1,12,612 x 0.81478 =   ₹919
Bucket 4: 0.008037 x 0.72 x   33,160 x 0.76099 =   ₹146
```

Results:

```
Stage 1 provision (12-month ECL) = ₹4,489 + ₹2,250 = ₹6,739  (2.10% of GCA)
Stage 2 provision (lifetime ECL) = ₹7,804                    (2.43% of GCA)
```

The Stage 2 uplift is only 16%. That is the seasoning effect: the loan has 22 months left, most of its default hazard is concentrated in the next twelve, and amortisation shrinks the exposure in the later buckets. Run the same calculation on a freshly disbursed 84-month home loan and the lifetime-to-12-month ratio is typically 4x to 7x, because the hazard is spread across a long horizon and the balance amortises slowly. The provisioning consequence of a Stage 2 transfer is therefore a function of product and vintage, not a fixed multiple.

Scenario weighting. Suppose the lifetime ECL is ₹7,804 in the base case, ₹6,120 in an upside case, and ₹15,340 in a downside case where unemployment rises 180 bps, with weights of 55%, 20%, and 25%:

```
ECL = 0.55 x 7,804 + 0.20 x 6,120 + 0.25 x 15,340
    = 4,292 + 1,224 + 3,835 = ₹9,351
```

The probability-weighted figure is 19.8% above the base case despite the base case carrying the majority weight. That gap is entirely a product of convexity, and it is the number an auditor will ask to see decomposed.

## How it connects

ECL assembles the same three parameters as 1.5, but requires them point-in-time, forward-looking, and discounted - a materially different calibration from the Basel IRB inputs in 1.10, which are through-the-cycle and downturn-based. The lifetime PD term structure comes from the vintage curves of 1.8 and the transition matrices of 1.9. The 30+ and 90+ DPD backstops map onto the delinquency buckets of 1.9. The macroeconomic scenario machinery is shared with credit stress testing (1.14). For Indian banks, the relationship with RBI IRAC provisioning norms (1.15) is a transition question with a defined date rather than a permanent dual regime.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Using 30+ DPD as the primary SICR trigger. It is a backstop. A model that only transfers at 30 DPD is running an incurred-loss framework with extra steps, and defeats the forward-looking intent.
- Comparing today's PD against an absolute threshold instead of against the origination expectation. A borrower who was originated at a 6% lifetime PD and is now at 6.5% has not experienced a significant increase; one originated at 1.2% and now at 3.0% has, despite the lower absolute level.
- Discounting at the current market rate. IFRS9 requires the original EIR. Using a current rate makes the provision move with interest rates for no credit reason.
- Applying contractual maturity to revolving products. Credit cards have a contractual notice period measured in days but a behavioural life measured in years. IFRS9 contains a specific exception requiring the behavioural life for such facilities, and using the contractual term collapses the provision to near nothing.
- Treating the scenario overlay as conservatism to be argued down. The convexity gap is a required output of the model, not a management buffer.
- Forgetting that Stage 3 interest accrues on the net carrying amount. Continuing to recognise gross interest on impaired assets inflates both income and the subsequent provision.
