# 1.8 Portfolio Segmentation and Vintage Analysis

## Definition

Segmentation is the practice of cutting a retail book into homogeneous groups - by product, geography, sourcing channel, score band, ticket size, customer tenure - so that risk can be measured where it actually varies rather than averaged into invisibility. **Vintage analysis** tracks each origination cohort separately across its own age, measuring cumulative bad rate at each month on book (MOB) rather than at each calendar month. The two techniques are used together: vintage curves cut by segment are the primary diagnostic tool in retail credit.

## Why it matters

Portfolio-level delinquency ratios are structurally misleading in a growing book. Because young accounts have not had time to default, rapid growth adds a large denominator of unseasoned exposure and pushes the reported ratio down - even while underwriting quality is deteriorating sharply. Vintage analysis removes this dilution by comparing cohorts at equal age. It is also the only feedback loop with a short enough latency to be actionable: MOB 3 delinquency on a cohort is observable four months after booking, whereas the full loss picture takes 18-24 months. If a lender is only looking at calendar-month portfolio ratios, it will discover a bad policy change roughly a year after it has stopped being fixable.

## Formula

```
Vintage bad rate (m, k) = Accounts from cohort m at 90+ DPD by MOB k / Accounts booked in cohort m
```

where:
- `m` = origination cohort, normally the booking month or quarter
- `k` = months on book, the age of the cohort at measurement
- The numerator is cumulative and typically "ever 90+ DPD", not "currently 90+", so that cures and write-offs do not distort the curve

The balance-weighted version substitutes disbursed amount for account count:

```
Vintage loss rate (m, k) = Cumulative balance ever 90+ DPD by MOB k / Original disbursed amount of cohort m
```

Both are used. Account-weighted curves reveal policy and scorecard problems; balance-weighted curves reveal limit and ticket-size problems. When the two diverge, the issue is concentrated in large-ticket accounts.

Useful derived measures:

```
Maturity ratio (k) = Vintage bad rate at MOB k / Vintage bad rate at MOB 24
```

This tells you how much of the eventual loss is visible at age k, allowing early cohorts to be projected forward. For unsecured personal loans, roughly 55-65% of lifetime 90+ incidence is typically visible by MOB 12.

## Worked Example

Example: personal loan cohorts, account-weighted cumulative ever-90+ DPD.

| Cohort | Accounts booked | MOB 3 | MOB 6 | MOB 9 | MOB 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 2025 | 8,420 | 0.21% | 0.94% | 1.86% | 2.71% |
| Apr 2025 | 11,650 | 0.28% | 1.31% | 2.54% | - |
| Jul 2025 | 15,900 | 0.41% | 1.88% | - | - |
| Oct 2025 | 19,240 | 0.57% | - | - | - |

Read down the MOB 3 column: 0.21%, 0.28%, 0.41%, 0.57%. Early delinquency has risen 2.7x across four quarterly cohorts. This is a first-payment and early-payment failure pattern, which points at origination and verification rather than at borrower stress - genuine macro deterioration shows up at MOB 6-12, not MOB 3.

Now the dilution effect. Observing the whole book in January 2026, each cohort sits at a different age:

```
Jan-25 at MOB 12:  8,420 x 2.71% =  228 accounts
Apr-25 at MOB 9:  11,650 x 2.54% =  296
Jul-25 at MOB 6:  15,900 x 1.88% =  299
Oct-25 at MOB 3:  19,240 x 0.57% =  110
                  ---------------------
Total:            55,210 accounts,   933 bad

Reported portfolio 90+ ratio = 933 / 55,210 = 1.69%
```

Compare against a counterfactual lender that booked the same four cohorts at a constant 8,420 accounts each, with these same deteriorating curves:

```
Bad accounts = 8,420 x (2.71% + 2.54% + 1.88% + 0.57%) = 648
Total accounts = 33,680
Reported ratio = 648 / 33,680 = 1.92%
```

The book growing at 23% per quarter reports 1.69%. The flat book with identical, equally deteriorating underwriting reports 1.92%. Growth alone produced a 23 bps improvement in the headline number while the underlying credit quality worsened in both. This is why portfolio delinquency ratios are not evidence of anything on their own.

Segment decomposition. Splitting the Oct-2025 cohort's 0.57% MOB 3 rate by channel:

| Channel | Share of cohort | MOB 3 ever-90+ |
|---|---|---|
| Digital / direct | 41% | 0.19% |
| Branch | 24% | 0.31% |
| DSA | 35% | 1.19% |

The portfolio-level deterioration is almost entirely a mix effect. DSA share rose from 18% in the Jan cohort to 35% in October, and DSA accounts fail early at roughly 6x the digital rate. The fix is channel-level cutoffs and commission clawbacks, not a blanket tightening that would suppress the profitable digital volume.

## How it connects

Vintage curves are the empirical validation of the PD estimates from 1.2 - if MOB 12 cumulative bad rates consistently exceed the scorecard's 12-month PD, the model is mis-calibrated and needs the central tendency adjustment described there. Segment-level curves justify the cutoff and pricing differentiation applied in 1.7. The seasoning pattern the curves reveal is the empirical basis for the lifetime PD term structure required under IFRS9 (1.11), and for the scenario overlays in stress testing (1.14). Roll-rate analysis (1.9) is the higher-frequency companion: vintages tell you which cohort is bad, roll rates tell you what is happening this month.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Comparing cohorts at different ages. The Oct-2025 cohort's 0.57% is not comparable with the Jan-2025 cohort's 2.71%. Only the MOB 3 column is a valid comparison, and only cohorts with a full k months of observation belong in column k.
- Segmenting until the cells are empty. A segment with 340 accounts and a 1.2% expected bad rate contains roughly four bad accounts. Its vintage curve is noise. Minimum cell sizes of 1,000-2,000 accounts are needed before a curve means anything.
- Using "currently 90+" instead of "ever 90+". Cures and write-offs remove accounts from the current count, flattening the curve and hiding losses that genuinely occurred.
- Ignoring the account-count versus balance-weighted divergence. If account vintages look stable but balance vintages deteriorate, the problem is in large-ticket approvals, and an account-only view will miss it entirely.
- Attributing a curve shift to macro conditions without checking mix. Channel, geography, and ticket-size mix shift far faster than borrower fundamentals. Decompose before diagnosing.
