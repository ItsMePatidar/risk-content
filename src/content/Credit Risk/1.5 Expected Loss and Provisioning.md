# 1.5 Expected Loss and Provisioning

## Definition

**Expected loss (EL)** is the average credit loss a lender should anticipate on an exposure over a defined horizon. It is the product of the three parameters already developed: the chance of default, the severity if default happens, and the amount at risk when it does. EL is a cost of doing business, not a surprise - it belongs in the loan price and in the provision. **Unexpected loss (UL)** is the volatility around that average, and is covered by capital rather than by pricing.

## Why it matters

The EL/UL split is the organising principle of the entire prudential framework. Price for EL, hold capital for UL. If a lender prices below EL it is booking guaranteed losses at scale, and volume growth accelerates the damage. If it provisions below EL, reported profit is fictitious and will be reclaimed by future write-offs. This is the mechanism behind most retail credit blow-ups: not a macro shock, but a product priced 150 bps under its own expected loss, growing at 50% per annum for eight quarters before the vintage curves make it visible.

## Formula

```
EL = PD x LGD x EAD
```

where:
- `EL` = expected loss over the horizon, in ₹
- `PD` = probability of default over that horizon, in % (12-month for annual EL, lifetime for IFRS9 Stage 2)
- `LGD` = loss given default, in % of exposure
- `EAD` = exposure at default, in ₹

Expressed as a rate, `EL% = PD x LGD`, since EAD is the base.

The pricing build-up converts EL into a minimum viable interest rate:

```
Breakeven yield = CoF + OPEX + EL% + [ K x (Re - CoF) ]
```

where:
- `CoF` = cost of funds, in % per annum
- `OPEX` = amortised acquisition plus servicing cost, in % of average assets
- `EL%` = expected loss rate, in % of average assets
- `K` = regulatory capital held against the exposure, as % of exposure, equal to the capital adequacy ratio multiplied by the applicable risk weight
- `Re` = required pre-tax return on equity, in %
- `(Re - CoF)` = the incremental cost of funding with equity instead of debt, in %

The three parameters must share a single default definition and horizon. Mixing a 90+ DPD PD with an LGD estimated on written-off accounts only, or a 12-month PD with a lifetime EAD, produces a number that means nothing.

## Worked Example

Example: pricing an unsecured personal loan product in India.

Parameters: PD 4.10% (12-month, 90+ DPD), LGD 72%, EAD taken as 100% of balance for an amortising loan.

```
EL% = 4.10% x 72% = 2.95% per annum
```

Capital charge. RBI applies a 125% risk weight to unsecured consumer credit; the lender runs a 15.5% total capital adequacy ratio against a 11.5% minimum, and targets a 16.0% pre-tax return on equity.

```
K = 15.5% x 125% = 19.38% of exposure
Capital charge = 19.38% x (16.0% - 7.90%) = 19.38% x 8.10% = 1.57% per annum
```

Build-up:

| Component | % p.a. |
|---|---|
| Cost of funds | 7.90% |
| Operating cost (0.53% acquisition + 1.85% servicing) | 2.38% |
| Expected loss | 2.95% |
| Capital charge | 1.57% |
| **Breakeven yield** | **14.80%** |

Against an actual all-in yield of 17.60% (16.50% interest plus 1.10% amortised fee), the product earns 280 bps above breakeven.

Sensitivity. Hold everything else constant and let PD drift from 4.10% to 5.80% - the kind of move produced by a channel-mix shift, not a recession:

```
EL% = 5.80% x 72% = 4.18%
Breakeven yield = 7.90% + 2.38% + 4.18% + 1.57% = 16.03%
```

The 280 bps cushion collapses to 157 bps. A further drift to 7.10% PD puts breakeven at 16.97% and the product is inside the noise band of its own pricing.

Portfolio scale. On a ₹1,840 crore book at the base parameters, annual expected loss is ₹1,840 crore x 2.95% = ₹54.3 crore. At the drifted PD it is ₹76.9 crore - a ₹22.6 crore swing that appears in the P&L roughly a year after the origination decisions that caused it.

## How it connects

EL is the assembly point for PD (1.2), LGD (1.3), and EAD (1.4), and the direct input to underwriting cutoffs and risk-based pricing (1.7). Its accounting expression is IFRS9 ECL (1.11), which differs from the formula above in three respects: it is discounted, it is lifetime for Stage 2 and Stage 3 assets, and it must be probability-weighted across macroeconomic scenarios. Its prudential expression is regulatory expected loss under IRB (1.10), which uses TTC PD and downturn LGD. Stress testing (1.14) is the exercise of re-computing EL under shocked parameters.

## Common Pitfalls / Misconceptions

- Treating EL as a worst case. It is an average. Roughly half of all outcomes are worse. The distribution's tail is UL, which is why capital exists alongside provisions.
- Double counting EL in pricing and capital. Regulatory capital under IRB covers unexpected loss only; the expected loss portion is deducted separately against provisions. Charging the full capital requirement and the full EL in the price overstates the breakeven rate.
- Multiplying parameters estimated on different populations. A PD from an application scorecard on through-the-door applicants and an LGD from a workout study on a five-year-old vintage are not compatible inputs.
- Assuming independence of PD and LGD. They are positively correlated - collateral values fall in the same conditions that raise default rates. This is precisely why downturn LGD is a separate regulatory requirement.
- Confusing provisions with expected loss. Provisions are an accounting measure governed by IFRS9 or RBI IRAC norms; EL is an economic measure. They will not tie out, and the reconciliation between them is a standing item in any credit risk review.
